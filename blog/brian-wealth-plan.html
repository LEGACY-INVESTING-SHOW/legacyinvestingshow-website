Brian's Financial Overview: Self-Employed Professional Wealth Building

Brian's wealth plan addresses the unique challenges and opportunities facing self-employed professionals — who have access to superior retirement contribution limits but face complex tax management, variable income, and additional tax burdens (self-employment tax).

Self-employment offers freedom and unlimited earning potential but demands financial discipline that employees don't face: quarterly tax payments, self-funded retirement, and no employer safety net.

Strategy 1: Solo 401(k) Maximization

The Solo 401(k) is the secret weapon for self-employed wealth building — contribution limits far exceeding IRA or even employee 401(k) options.

Solo 401(k) Contribution Mechanics

Dual Contribution Structure:

Employee Deferral:

  • 2025 limit: $23,000 ($30,500 if age 50+)
  • Can be Traditional (deductible) or Roth (post-tax)
  • 100% of net self-employment income up to limit

Employer Contribution:

  • 25% of net self-employment income
  • Calculated on income after self-employment tax deduction
  • Maximum combined: $69,000 ($76,500 if age 50+)

Calculation Example:

  • Gross business income: $150,000
  • Business expenses: $30,000
  • Net before SE tax: $120,000
  • Self-employment tax (15.3% × 92.35%): ~$16,960
  • Net SE income: $103,040
  • Employer contribution max: $103,040 × 25% = $25,760
  • Plus employee deferral: $23,000
  • Total contribution: $48,760

Solo 401(k) Implementation Timeline

Setup (Month 1):

  1. Open Solo 401(k) at low-cost provider (Fidelity, Schwab, E*Trade)
  2. Execute plan documents (adoption agreement)
  3. Set up automatic transfers from business account

Ongoing Contributions:

  • Monthly: Employee deferral ($1,917/month for $23,000 annual)
  • Quarterly: Employer contribution based on profit
  • Annual: True-up calculation at year-end

Provider Comparison:

Provider Setup Fee Annual Fee Investment Options Roth Option
Fidelity $0 $0 Full brokerage Yes
Schwab $0 $0 Full brokerage Yes
E*Trade $0 $0 Full brokerage Yes
Vanguard $0 $20 Vanguard funds Yes

Recommendation: Fidelity or Schwab for full brokerage flexibility at zero cost.

Roth vs. Traditional Solo 401(k)

Choose Traditional if:

  • Current bracket > expected retirement bracket
  • Need immediate deduction for cash flow
  • Reducing AGI for other tax benefits (ACA subsidies, etc.)

Choose Roth if:

  • Current bracket < expected retirement bracket
  • Long time horizon (25+ years)
  • Want tax-free growth and withdrawals
  • Already have significant Traditional balances

Brian's Hybrid Approach:

  • 50% Traditional employer contributions (deductible)
  • 50% Roth employee deferrals (tax-free growth)
  • Result: Tax flexibility in retirement

Strategy 2: Self-Employment Tax Management

Self-employed professionals pay both halves of FICA (15.3% up to Social Security wage base, 2.9% Medicare on all income).

Understanding Self-Employment Tax

Components:

  • Social Security: 12.4% on first $168,600 (2025 wage base)
  • Medicare: 2.9% on all self-employment income
  • Additional Medicare: 0.9% on income >$200,000 single / $250,000 MFJ
  • Total: 15.3% up to wage base, then 2.9%+

Calculation Base:

  • 92.35% of net self-employment income
  • Adjusts for employer half deduction

Example:

  • Net SE income: $100,000
  • Taxable base: $92,350
  • SE tax: $92,350 × 15.3% = $14,130
  • Deductible (employer half): $7,065

S-Corporation Tax Savings Strategy

When to Elect S-Corp:

  • Net business income consistently $40,000-$50,000+
  • SE tax savings exceed compliance costs (~$1,500-$2,000/year)

How It Works:

  • Pay yourself "reasonable salary" (subject to payroll taxes)
  • Take remaining profit as distribution (not subject to SE tax)

Example Savings:

Net Income Salary Distribution SE Tax (Sole Prop) Payroll Tax (S-Corp) Savings
$80,000 $40,000 $40,000 $12,240 $6,120 $6,120
$120,000 $55,000 $65,000 $18,360 $8,415 $9,945
$150,000 $70,000 $80,000 $20,520 $10,710 $9,810

S-Corp Implementation:

  1. Form LLC (if not already)
  2. File Form 2553 for S-Corp election (by March 15 for current year)
  3. Set up payroll (Gusto, ADP, or payroll service)
  4. Pay reasonable salary with proper documentation
  5. File 1120-S and K-1 annually

Reasonable Salary Determination:

  • Market rate for services rendered
  • Industry benchmarking data
  • Time tracking and employment agreement
  • 50-60% of net income typically defensible

Strategy 3: Quarterly Estimated Tax Management

Self-employed professionals must make quarterly estimated tax payments covering both income and self-employment taxes.

Quarterly Payment Schedule

Due Dates:

  • Q1: April 15 (prior year tax due as well)
  • Q2: June 15
  • Q3: September 15
  • Q4: January 15 (of following year)

Payment Methods:

  • EFTPS.gov (Electronic Federal Tax Payment System)
  • IRS Direct Pay (bank transfer)
  • Check with Form 1040-ES voucher

Safe Harbor Rules (Avoid Penalties)

To avoid underpayment penalties, pay:

  • 100% of prior year tax liability (110% if AGI >$150,000), OR
  • 90% of current year tax liability

Safe Harbor Strategy:

  • Use prior year safe harbor when income increasing
  • Calculate 90% of current year if income decreasing significantly
  • Benefit: No need to estimate perfectly; safe harbor protects from penalties

State Estimated Taxes:

  • Most states have similar estimated payment requirements
  • Due dates may differ from federal
  • Don't forget: State penalties also apply

Quarterly Cash Flow Planning

Monthly Reserve Calculation:

  1. Calculate average monthly net income (after business expenses)
  2. Estimate effective tax rate (federal + state + SE tax)
  • Typically 25-40% depending on income and state
  1. Set aside tax reserve monthly: Income × Tax Rate

Example:

  • Monthly net income: $10,000
  • Estimated effective rate: 30%
  • Monthly tax reserve: $3,000
  • Quarterly payment: $9,000

Separate Tax Account:

  • Dedicated savings account for tax reserves
  • Auto-transfer monthly reserve amount
  • Never commingle with operating funds
  • Pay quarterly from this account

Strategy 4: Maximizing Business Deductions

Self-employed professionals have extensive deduction opportunities — Brian's plan maximizes legitimate business expenses.

High-Value Deductions

Home Office:

  • Must be exclusive use for business (dedicated space)
  • Simplified method: $5/sq ft up to 300 sq ft ($1,500 max)
  • Actual expense method: Percentage of rent/mortgage, utilities, insurance
  • Requirements: Regular use, principal place of business

Vehicle Expenses:

  • Standard mileage rate: $0.67/mile (2024-2025)
  • Or actual expenses (gas, maintenance, depreciation) × business %
  • Tracking: Mileage log mandatory (app or written)
  • Common trips: Client meetings, business errands, conferences

Health Insurance:

  • 100% deductible for self, spouse, dependents
  • Premiums deducted "above the line" (not itemized)
  • Limitation: Can't deduct if eligible for employer plan (through spouse)

Professional Development:

  • Courses, conferences, certifications
  • Books, subscriptions, professional memberships
  • Travel for professional development
  • Requirement: Maintain or improve skills in current business

Retirement Contributions:

  • Solo 401(k), SEP-IRA contributions
  • Deductible as business expense
  • Reduces both income and SE tax

Business Meals:

  • 50% deductible
  • Must be business purpose, documented
  • Change: Entertainment no longer deductible (post-TCJA)

Documentation Requirements

Receipt Management:

  • Digital copies acceptable (photo/scan)
  • Must show amount, date, vendor, business purpose
  • Rule: No receipt = no deduction if audited
  • Tools: Expensify, Receipt Bank, Shoeboxed

Mileage Tracking:

  • Date, destination, purpose, miles
  • Apps: MileIQ, Everlance, TripLog
  • Contemporaneous: Log within 24 hours

Home Office:

  • Measure square footage
  • Photograph space (exclusively business use)
  • Document if audited

Strategy 5: Health Insurance and Benefits Optimization

Self-employed professionals self-fund benefits that employees receive from employers — Brian's plan optimizes these costs.

Health Insurance Strategy

Marketplace (ACA) Plans:

  • Healthcare.gov or state exchange
  • Premium tax credits if income <400% FPL
  • Self-employed advantage: Can deduct premiums AND receive subsidies (if income qualifies)

Health Savings Account (HSA) Strategy:

  • Must be enrolled in HDHP (High Deductible Health Plan)
  • Triple tax advantage: deductible contributions, tax-free growth, tax-free medical
  • 2025 limits: $4,300 individual / $8,550 family + $1,000 catch-up (55+)
  • Best retirement account if eligible

HSA as Retirement Strategy:

  • Maximize contributions
  • Invest HSA funds (don't keep in cash)
  • Pay current medical expenses out of pocket
  • Save receipts for future tax-free reimbursement
  • Result: Tax-free in, tax-free growth, tax-free out

Other Self-Employed Benefits

Disability Insurance:

  • Essential (no employer-provided coverage)
  • Target: 60% of income replacement
  • Elimination period: 90 days (lower premium) vs. 30 days
  • Cost: 1-3% of income

Life Insurance:

  • Term life: 10-20× income
  • Duration: Until financially independent or dependents self-sufficient
  • Cost: $20-50/month for healthy 30-40 year old ($500K-$1M coverage)

Liability Insurance:

  • Professional liability (errors & omissions)
  • General liability (if clients visit office)
  • Umbrella policy: $1M-$2M minimum

Key Takeaways: Lessons from Brian's Self-Employed Plan

1. Solo 401(k) Is the Self-Employed Superpower

The $69,000 Solo 401(k) capacity dwarfs IRA options available to employees. Self-employed professionals who don't maximize this leave tens of thousands in tax benefits on the table annually.

2. Quarterly Tax Management Is Non-Negotiable

Unlike employees who have taxes withheld, self-employed professionals must actively manage quarterly payments. Underpayment penalties plus interest make poor planning expensive.

3. S-Corporation Election Is Worth It at the Right Income

The self-employment tax savings from S-Corp status ($6,000-$10,000 annually) justify compliance costs once income reaches $40,000-$50,000. Brian's plan evaluates this threshold carefully.

4. Business Deductions Require Discipline

The difference between self-employed professionals who capture legitimate deductions and those who don't is thousands in taxes. Documentation systems matter as much as deduction knowledge.

5. Self-Funded Benefits Need Optimization

Without employer-provided benefits, health insurance, disability, and life insurance must be self-selected. HSA strategies, marketplace subsidies, and proper coverage levels require active management.

Frequently Asked Questions About Self-Employed Strategies

How do I know if S-Corp is worth it for my income?

Break-even calculation:

  • SE tax savings: ~15% of (net income - reasonable salary)
  • Compliance cost: $1,500-$2,000/year (payroll, tax prep)
  • Break-even: When SE tax savings exceed compliance costs

Examples:

  • $40,000 net: ~$3,000 savings - $2,000 cost = $1,000 net (marginal)
  • $60,000 net: ~$4,500 savings - $2,000 cost = $2,500 net (worth it)
  • $100,000 net: ~$6,750 savings - $2,000 cost = $4,750 net (definitely worth it)

What if my income is too variable for quarterly estimates?

Variable income strategies:

  • Use annualized income installment method (calculates actual quarterly)
  • Overpay Q1-Q2 based on conservative estimates, adjust Q3-Q4
  • Use safe harbor (prior year) and true up at year-end
  • Better to overpay slightly than underpay and face penalties

Can I have a Solo 401(k) and a side job 401(k)?

Employee deferral limit is aggregated:

  • Total employee deferral across all 401(k)s: $23,000
  • Example: $10,000 to employer 401(k) + $13,000 to Solo 401(k) = $23,000 total

Employer contribution is separate:

  • Solo 401(k) employer contribution: 25% of SE income (separate limit)
  • Result: Can maximize even with side job

Should I hire my spouse to increase retirement contributions?

If spouse legitimately works in business:

  • Can hire and pay reasonable salary
  • Spouse can contribute to Solo 401(k) too
  • Double the retirement capacity: $69,000 × 2 = $138,000 total
  • Requirements: Actual work, reasonable compensation, proper documentation

How do I handle health insurance if my spouse has employer coverage?

Options:

  1. Join spouse's employer plan (often most cost-effective)
  2. Decline employer coverage (if allowed), purchase individual HDHP for HSA eligibility
  3. Keep employer coverage, don't establish HSA

Note: If eligible for employer coverage (even if declined), can't deduct individual premiums.

Brian's plan maximizes the Solo 401(k), which allows contributions up to $69,000 annually (2025). This includes $23,000 as employee deferral plus 25% of net self-employment income as employer contribution. For high-earning self-employed professionals, this dwarfs the $7,000 IRA limit available to employees. The key is calculating the maximum employer contribution while maintaining sufficient cash flow for operations and taxes.

Self-employed individuals must pay estimated taxes quarterly (April 15, June 15, September 15, January 15) covering both income tax and self-employment tax (15.3% on 92.35% of net earnings up to Social Security cap). Brian's plan uses the safe harbor method (paying 100% of prior year tax or 110% if income exceeds $150,000) to avoid underpayment penalties while managing cash flow. Quarterly planning sessions ensure adequate reserves and accurate projections.

Self-employment tax (15.3% covering Social Security and Medicare) can be reduced through S-Corporation election once net income reaches $40,000-$50,000. By splitting income between salary (subject to payroll taxes) and distributions (not subject to self-employment tax), the effective rate on distribution portion drops significantly. Brian's plan evaluates this election at appropriate income thresholds while considering additional compliance costs.

High-value deductions include: home office (percentage of housing costs for dedicated workspace), vehicle expenses (mileage or actual costs for business travel), professional development (courses, conferences, certifications), health insurance premiums (100% deductible for self-employed), retirement contributions (Solo 401k, SEP-IRA), and business meals (50% deductible). Meticulous documentation separates legitimate business expenses from personal costs.

Brian's plan recommends LLC formation immediately for liability protection, then S-Corporation election once net income reaches $40,000-$50,000 annually where tax savings exceed compliance costs. The LLC provides legal protection and operational flexibility at minimal cost, while S-Corp status reduces self-employment tax burden. Sole proprietorship leaves personal assets exposed to business liabilities and forgoes significant tax optimization opportunities.

Section 199A provides a 20% deduction on qualified business income for pass-through entities including sole proprietors, LLCs, and S-Corps. For self-employed professionals, this effectively reduces tax rate on business income. Brian's plan maximizes this by ensuring adequate W-2 wages if the wage limitation applies, and avoiding specified service trade or business phase-outs if income exceeds thresholds. The deduction currently sunsets after 2025, making it a limited-time optimization opportunity.
