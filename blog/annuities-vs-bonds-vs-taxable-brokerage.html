annuities vs bonds vs taxable brokerage: Which Strategy Works Better in 2026?

Compare annuities, bond ladders, and taxable brokerage accounts for 2026 retirement income using practical tax math, allocation frameworks, and a 30-day action checklist.

If you are planning retirement income in 2026, annuities vs bonds vs taxable brokerage is the core allocation decision. Most households do better when they stop asking which single product is best and start assigning each tool a specific job: guaranteed income, capital stability, and flexible growth. This approach can reduce regret when rates change, markets dip, or spending spikes.

A practical way to frame it: annuities can transfer longevity risk to an insurer, bonds can fund known cash needs, and taxable brokerage can give liquidity and favorable long-term capital-gains treatment. annuities vs bonds vs taxable brokerage: a practical 2026 decision framework

Use four inputs before you buy anything:

  1. Essential annual spending: housing, food, insurance, healthcare, and taxes.
  2. Guaranteed income: Social Security, pension, rental net income.
  3. Liquidity target: cash you need available within 24 months.
  4. Flex spending target: travel, gifting, hobbies, and discretionary goals.

Then calculate your income floor gap:

Income floor gap = Essential spending - Guaranteed income

If the gap is large and you are worried about living into your 90s, annuities usually deserve a bigger role. If the gap is small, you can lean more on bonds and taxable brokerage.

Next, assign portfolio jobs:

  • Floor bucket: cover essential spending with high confidence.
  • Stability bucket: reduce drawdown risk over the next 2 to 7 years.
  • Flexibility bucket: fund discretionary spending and legacy goals.

A useful default for many retirees is not 100/0 decisions. It is a range approach, such as 20% to 40% annuity, 20% to 40% bonds, and 30% to 60% taxable brokerage, then adjusted for taxes and risk tolerance.

What each tool actually does in your plan

Annuities

Annuities are insurance contracts. They can provide lifetime cash flow and help protect against sequence-of-returns risk when combined with equities. The key tradeoff is liquidity and complexity.

Pros:

  • Can create predictable monthly income.
  • Can hedge longevity risk.
  • Can improve behavior for people who overspend during bull markets.

Cons:

  • Fees, riders, and surrender charges can be significant.
  • Inflation protection is limited unless you pay for it.
  • Less liquid than bonds or brokerage assets.

Tax note:

  • Qualified annuities funded with pre-tax money are generally taxed as ordinary income on distributions.
  • Nonqualified annuities may use an exclusion ratio after annuitization, so part of each payment can be return of principal.

Bonds

Bonds are debt instruments. They are useful when you need known principal value at known dates, especially through ladders.

Pros:

  • Predictable maturity value when held to maturity.
  • Good for 1- to 10-year spending needs.
  • Treasuries carry low credit risk.

Cons:

  • Reinvestment risk when rates fall.
  • Inflation can erode real income.
  • Bond funds can decline when yields rise.

Tax note:

  • Most bond interest is taxed as ordinary income.
  • Treasury interest is usually exempt from state income tax.
  • Municipal bonds can be attractive in high state and federal tax brackets.

Taxable brokerage

Taxable brokerage is a flexible account structure, not a single investment. Fidelity's learning-center guidance often emphasizes it for nonretirement goals, no early withdrawal penalties, and investing after tax-advantaged accounts are largely funded.

Pros:

  • Full liquidity and no age-based withdrawal penalties.
  • Potentially lower tax rates on qualified dividends and long-term gains.
  • Step-up in basis potential for heirs under current rules.

Cons:

  • No upfront tax deduction.
  • Ongoing tax drag from dividends, interest, and realized gains.
  • Behavior risk if investors trade too frequently.

Fully worked numeric example with assumptions and tradeoffs

Assumptions for a married couple, both age 62, retirement at 65:

  • Investable portfolio: $1,200,000 (outside home equity).
  • Essential spending goal at age 67: $96,000 per year after inflation adjustments.
  • Expected Social Security at 67: $54,000 per year combined.
  • Income floor gap at 67: $42,000 per year.
  • Federal tax assumptions: 24% ordinary bracket, 15% qualified dividends and long-term gains.
  • They want meaningful liquidity for healthcare surprises and family support.

Proposed allocation at age 65:

  • $350,000 to a joint-life immediate annuity beginning at 67 with 10-year certain period, estimated payout 6.3%: $22,050 per year.
  • $250,000 to a 5-year Treasury and high-grade bond ladder, expected blended yield 4.6%: $11,500 per year interest.
  • $600,000 to taxable brokerage with low-turnover equity ETFs and short/intermediate municipal bond ETF, expected dividend yield about 2.0%: $12,000 per year dividends.

After-tax cash flow estimate at age 67:

  1. Annuity cash flow:
  • Gross payment: $22,050
  • Assume nonqualified exclusion ratio where 35% is taxable as ordinary income.
  • Taxable amount: $7,718
  • Estimated federal tax: $1,852
  • After-tax annuity cash flow: $20,198
  1. Bond ladder cash flow:
  • Interest: $11,500
  • Estimated tax at 24%: $2,760
  • After-tax bond cash flow: $8,740
  1. Brokerage dividends:
  • Dividends: $12,000
  • Estimated tax at 15%: $1,800
  • After-tax dividends: $10,200
  1. Supplemental taxable sale for spending flexibility:
  • Planned sale: $8,000
  • Assume 20% embedded gain ratio, so taxable gain is $1,600.
  • Estimated tax at 15%: $240
  • Net proceeds after tax: $7,760

Total projected after-tax portfolio cash flow:

  • $20,198 + $8,740 + $10,200 + $7,760 = $46,898 per year.

Result:

  • Income floor gap was $42,000.
  • Portfolio strategy projects about $4,898 annual buffer above the gap.

Tradeoffs to understand:

  • If interest rates fall, future bond reinvestment income may drop.
  • If inflation stays elevated, fixed annuity purchasing power can decline.
  • If equities drop early in retirement, brokerage withdrawals may need temporary cuts.
  • If the couple wants more legacy value, they may lower annuity size and raise brokerage allocation.

Tax mechanics that usually decide the winner

For many US households, taxes are the swing factor in annuities vs bonds vs taxable brokerage.

  1. Ordinary vs capital-gain rate spread
  • Bond interest and most qualified-annuity withdrawals are ordinary income.
  • Qualified dividends and long-term gains in taxable brokerage can be taxed at lower rates.
  1. Asset location matters
  • Holding taxable bonds in tax-deferred accounts can reduce annual tax drag.
  • Holding broad equity index ETFs in taxable accounts can improve after-tax efficiency due to low turnover.
  1. Municipal bonds can change the math
  • In high tax brackets, municipal bond funds in taxable accounts may beat taxable bond funds on an after-tax basis.
  1. Annuity tax treatment differs by funding source
  • Pre-tax funded annuities: generally fully taxable as ordinary income.
  • After-tax funded annuities: part of each annuitized payment may be principal return.
  1. RMD interaction
  • Large pre-tax balances can create taxable income pressure in your 70s.
  • A balanced taxable sleeve can give withdrawal flexibility when RMDs are high.

Fidelity's framework on taxable brokerage is practical here: use it for goals that need flexibility and for additional investing once tax-advantaged capacity is largely used. Pair this with your rollover plan using 401(k) rollover guide.

Step-by-step implementation plan

  1. Define your income floor gap
  • Calculate essential spending.
  • Subtract guaranteed income sources.
  • Set a target floor coverage percentage, such as 80% to 100%.
  1. Build a spending timeline
  • Years 1 to 2: cash and very short Treasuries.
  • Years 3 to 7: bond ladder.
  • Years 8+: growth-oriented taxable brokerage sleeve.
  1. Set your annuity ceiling
  • Many households cap annuity allocation at a level that still leaves emergency flexibility, often 20% to 40% of investable assets.
  1. Collect comparable annuity quotes
  • Request at least three carrier quotes on the same rider assumptions.
  • Evaluate payout rate, inflation options, surrender terms, and carrier ratings.
  1. Construct the bond ladder intentionally
  • Match maturity dates to expected withdrawals.
  • Diversify issuer quality.
  • Avoid overconcentration in long-duration bonds if cash flow is near-term.
  1. Build taxable brokerage for tax efficiency
  • Use low-turnover ETFs for equities.
  • Keep a rules-based rebalancing threshold.
  • Plan tax-loss harvesting rules before volatility arrives.
  1. Create withdrawal rules
  • Use dividends and bond interest first.
  • Refill cash bucket from appreciated assets during strong markets.
  • Pause discretionary spending increases after large drawdowns.
  1. Run an annual tax and risk review
  • Recheck bracket exposure, RMD projections, and Medicare premium thresholds.
  • Coordinate with your CPA and advisor before year-end trades.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

  1. Buying an annuity before defining the floor gap
  • Fix: solve spending first, product second.
  1. Chasing yield with long-duration or lower-quality bonds
  • Fix: match bond duration to spending horizon and required certainty.
  1. Ignoring tax drag in taxable brokerage
  • Fix: favor low-turnover funds and harvest losses when appropriate.
  1. Treating annuities vs bonds as binary
  • Fix: use bucket roles and combine tools intentionally.
  1. Over-annuitizing and then needing liquidity
  • Fix: preserve a meaningful liquid reserve for healthcare and family events.
  1. Running no downside scenario
  • Fix: test at least one recession-year withdrawal path before retiring.
  1. Forgetting sequence risk in early retirement
  • Fix: use bond/cash buffers so you do not sell equities after sharp declines.
  1. Skipping annual updates
  • Fix: revisit tax brackets, spending, and allocation every year.

When Not to Use This Strategy

This strategy is weaker if any of the following apply:

  • You have very small investable assets and need maximum simplicity.
  • You have major high-interest debt that should be eliminated first.
  • Your health outlook suggests low value from longevity insurance.
  • You need near-total liquidity in the next 3 years for known expenses.
  • You cannot commit to annual reviews and tax coordination.
  • You are still in accumulation mode and have not yet optimized employer plans.

If those describe you, simplify first. Start with debt cleanup, emergency reserves, and core retirement account optimization. Then revisit annuities vs bonds vs taxable brokerage later.

Questions to Ask Your CPA/Advisor

  1. Based on my current and projected tax brackets, what is my after-tax withdrawal order?
  2. How would this allocation affect Medicare IRMAA exposure over the next 5 years?
  3. Should I hold taxable bonds in pre-tax accounts and equities in taxable accounts?
  4. What annuity tax treatment applies in my case: qualified, nonqualified, or mixed?
  5. What is my expected RMD trajectory at ages 73, 75, and beyond?
  6. Under what market drawdown should I reduce discretionary withdrawals?
  7. How much liquidity should remain outside annuity contracts?
  8. What stress test assumptions are you using for inflation, returns, and sequence risk?
  9. How will this plan affect legacy goals and beneficiary tax outcomes?
  10. What specific triggers would cause us to rebalance or adjust payout sources?

Bring written assumptions into the meeting. Good advice depends on concrete inputs, not generic risk questionnaires.

Final takeaways for 2026 retirees

The strongest plans usually combine tools. Use annuities for baseline confidence, bonds for near-term spending certainty, and taxable brokerage for flexibility, tax control, and legacy options. Keep the process rules-based: define your floor gap, model after-tax cash flow, and review annually.
