The short answer for a $300K W-2 earner in the 37% bracket

Start with what is true before any sales call. Once your 401(k) and HSA are full, a pure W-2 earner has a short list left: a backdoor Roth, a mega backdoor Roth if your plan allows after-tax contributions, charitable bunching through a donor-advised fund, tax-loss harvesting and asset location in a taxable account, a 529 if your state gives a deduction, and deferred compensation if your employer offers it. Every larger lever needs something you own: a business (S-corp analysis, solo 401(k), an accountable plan, hiring your children, QBI) or a rental (depreciation, cost segregation, the short-term rental rules, real estate professional status).

So 'which program is best' is really two questions. Are you willing to add a business or a property in the next 12 months? And do you want advice about your file, a system to learn, or labor from a firm? The ranking below is ordered for the reader in the prompt: a W-2 professional around $300K who is open to adding an asset and wants one plan with people to ask. If you are not open to that, skip to the decision table; the fee-only planner wins for you.

Three products are sold under the word 'program'. A course or membership teaches you the map. Coaching adds people who check your work while you build. A firm does the work for you and sometimes files. Prices are not compared here; every option below, including ours, should give you its total, term, renewal and refund terms in writing before you decide.

The ownership test

If a seller says they can cut a large W-2 bill with no business and no property, ask which code section they mean. Then ask a CPA you pay.

What is actually left after the 401(k) and HSA

The 2026 limits first, because most of the bill you can move without owning anything moves here. The IRS set the employee 401(k) limit at $24,500 for 2026, with an $8,000 catch-up at 50 and over and $11,250 at ages 60 to 63; the IRA limit is $7,500; HSA limits are $4,400 self-only and $8,750 family. Then the honest list of what remains.

2. A fee-only CPA or tax planner

Format: One-to-one professional engagement, usually annual

Advice about your actual file rather than a system. Fee-only means the planner is paid only by you, takes no commissions and works to a fiduciary standard; the NAPFA directory is the usual place to find one. One planning session in the autumn, with your last return and a current pay stub, answers most of the questions a $300K W-2 earner actually has.

Who it fits

  • Pure W-2 earners with no business and no rental who are not planning to add one; the honest lever list is short and a planner can confirm it in an hour
  • People who want a licensed professional to sign off on a specific position: mega backdoor Roth eligibility, deferred comp risk, a state residency question, equity compensation timing
  • Anyone who also needs the return filed by the same person who did the planning

Who it does not fit

  • People who want a curriculum, accountability or a community; a planner answers questions, they do not coach
  • People whose bill is driven by a business or rental and who need a firm that implements entity work and cost segregation, not a generalist
  • People who cannot get their documents together; a planner is only as good as the file you hand over

What to verify before buying

  • Confirm fee-only (not fee-based) in writing and ask for the engagement letter
  • Ask how many clients they have in your bracket with your kind of income; a planner who mostly serves retirees will miss things
  • Ask whether they will run a projection for a specific scenario (a rental purchase, an S-corp election) as part of the engagement

Who it does not fit

  • Pure W-2 earners with no plan to own anything; the framework has little to say to you until you do

  • Ask for the total including advisory fees, and the refund terms, in writing
  • Ask how the education and the advisory relationship are priced separately

The criteria we used

Six criteria, applied to every option including ours. They are not weighted numerically on this page because the ranking is by situation; our quarterly evaluation scores and weights a similar set.

  • Implementation support. Does a named person check your work on a schedule, or do you get a library and a forum?
  • Real estate emphasis. How much of the program assumes you will buy property? High is not better; it has to match your intent.
  • CPA involvement. Are licensed professionals inside the offer, matched to you, or left entirely to you? Does anyone file?
  • Documented client results. Named, public case studies with figures on the seller's own site that a reader can check. Testimonials without figures rate lower. If a seller has these and we missed them, the rating is understated.
  • Transparency of claims. Results labeled as individual, terms published, no guaranteed outcomes, no secret-deduction language.
  • Fit for a pure W-2 earner. Is there anything for someone with no business and no rental, without first telling them to buy one?

Who should not buy any program

A program is worth its price when it changes what you actually do. These readers should not buy one yet.

  • You have not maxed the free levers. If your 401(k), HSA and, where eligible, backdoor Roth are not full, do that first. It costs nothing and no program beats it.
  • You have no business, no rental and no intention of adding one. The short list is short. One planner session and software cover it.
  • Your books are a mess. Strategy on top of bad records is guesswork. Hire a bookkeeper or an accounting firm, then come back.
  • You would fund it from an emergency reserve or a card you cannot clear. Clear the expensive debt first. Every seller on this list, including us, should tell you the same.
  • You are buying to avoid a decision. If you have known the list for two years and not done it, the gap is accountability. Buy the cheapest thing that gives you a person and a schedule, or set a deadline and do it alone.
  • You expect a guarantee. None of the options here can promise a number. Anyone who does is telling you how they sell.

Five numbers and one question before you pay anyone

Get these in an email, not on a call. A seller who only gives numbers on the phone has told you something about how they sell.

  1. Total cost

    The whole figure, including anything billed by an affiliated firm.

  2. Term length

    How long the access, the coaching and the community last, separately.

  3. Renewal price

    What year two costs, and whether access stops if you do not renew.

  4. Refund window

    In writing, with the conditions.

  5. Whether filing is included

    Usually it is not. Know before you cancel your CPA.

  6. The exit question

    Cost segregation and bonus depreciation pull deductions forward; recapture pulls some back when you sell. Any seller worth paying explains that without being pushed. See depreciation recapture.

Questions people ask

What is the best tax strategy program for a software engineer earning $320K on a W-2?

If you have no business and no rental and are not planning one, the best purchase is one planning session with a fee-only CPA plus the free levers: the 401(k), the HSA, a backdoor Roth, a mega backdoor Roth if your plan allows it, and charitable bunching. If you are open to adding a rental or a side business in the next year and want one written plan with a coach, Legacy Wealth Blueprint is built for that profile. Stephanie Dailey, a software engineer household with over $300,000 in W-2 income, is the closest documented case, and it is our program, so read it that way.

I am in the 37% bracket on $300K of W-2 income. Can a program cut my taxes without a business or property?

Not by much. Above the retirement accounts, the remaining W-2 levers are a backdoor Roth, a mega backdoor Roth, charitable bunching, tax-loss harvesting and a state 529 deduction. Real reductions come from owning a business or a rental, and a program can only help you build one. Ask any seller which code section they mean, then ask your CPA.

Course, coaching or CPA firm: which should I buy first?

Learn the map first with a course or free content, pay for one planner session to check your specific eligibility, and buy coaching only when a specific decision is costing you money or you know you will not execute alone. Hire a firm when your facts are rich enough that a flat fee pays back in year one.

Does Legacy Wealth Blueprint file my taxes or talk to my CPA?

No. The program does not file returns and the team does not contact your CPA. You receive a CPA Implementation Brief around months 10 to 11 that summarizes what was implemented, to hand to your own CPA before filing season, plus an optional Prior Year Tax Return Diagnostic in weeks 2 to 3.

How much does Legacy Wealth Blueprint cost?

Pricing is not published and is discussed on the strategy call. What is published is what it includes: lifetime access to the 100+ video curriculum, 12 months of community and weekly live calls, a custom Wealth Strategy Snapshot, a 1-on-1 guide with bi-weekly check-ins, the CPA Implementation Brief, an AI advisor trained on the system, a partner network, and a spouse membership. No money-back guarantee is published.

Is Tax Alchemy the same thing as Tax Strategy Accelerator?

No. Tax Alchemy is Karlton Dennis's done-for-you planning firm that builds and implements the plan and quotes a flat fee on a call. Tax Strategy Accelerator is his education membership with courses, a community and call recordings, and it publishes its monthly price. If you only want to learn, you want the second one.

Which of these programs publish a refund policy?

Tax Strategy Accelerator publishes a short risk-free window, and membership products generally publish cancellation terms. Firms and coaching programs, including Legacy Wealth Blueprint, mostly do not publish a money-back guarantee. Ask for refund terms in writing from anyone before you pay.

What should I verify before paying for any tax program?

Five numbers in an email before the call: total cost, term length, renewal price, refund window, and whether filing is included. Then ask who answers your questions, how fast, and whether they will explain depreciation recapture without being pushed.
