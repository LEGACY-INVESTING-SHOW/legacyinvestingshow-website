Tax strategies for physicians

For most physicians the best tax strategy is the plain one: fill every pre-tax account the employer offers, including a 457(b) if the hospital is a nonprofit, add an HSA and a backdoor Roth, and stop there unless there is other income. Moonlighting or locums pay unlocks a solo 401(k) and possibly an S-corp. Rental losses that offset physician wages usually need a spouse who can meet the participation tests, not the physician.

A physician’s income is mostly W-2, mostly taxed at the top rates, and the specified service rules take away the deductions a business owner would lean on. So the moves below are grouped by the facts that unlock them: payroll alone, a 1099 income stream, a spouse with time, or a single high-income year.

How the moves are grouped

Grouped by what each one asks of you, not by the size of the deduction. Work down from the top of the first group. Some can be put in place during the year. Some need an account opened before money moves. Some only work if the documentation exists before the deduction is claimed.

Employed W-2 physician

Hospital and health-system physicians get the same short list as any W-2 earner, with one addition. Many nonprofit employers offer a 457(b) next to the 403(b), and the two have separate deferral limits, so both can be filled in the same year. Read the plan document first if it is a non-governmental 457(b): the balance stays an asset of the employer until it is paid out, and the payout schedule is fixed when you separate. If the plan accepts after-tax contributions and allows in-plan conversion, the mega backdoor Roth is the largest remaining account move. Pre-tax deferrals also lower the adjusted gross income that sets an income-driven student loan payment, which matters if you are counting on PSLF, and a Roth conversion or a joint return does the reverse.

1. Health savings account

A Health Savings Account (HSA) offers a triple tax advantage: contributions are tax-deductible, growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free. Many investors use HSAs as stealth retirement accounts by paying current medical expenses out-of-pocket and letting the HSA grow.

Potential savings Triple tax benefit with annual contribution limits
Best fit Healthy individuals with high-deductible health plans
Level beginner
Typical cost Free with most providers

2. Mega backdoor Roth

Uses after-tax 401(k) contributions and in-plan conversion to add Roth dollars above the deferral limit.

Level advanced

3. Backdoor Roth IRA

Reaches a Roth IRA through a non-deductible contribution and conversion when income exceeds the Roth limits.

Level intermediate

4. Dependent care FSA

Pays childcare and dependent care costs with pre-tax payroll dollars.

Level beginner

5. Tax loss harvesting

Sells losing positions to offset realized gains while respecting the wash-sale rule.

Level beginner

1099 moonlighting or locums income

Any self-employment income opens a second set of moves. A solo 401(k) takes both an employee deferral and an employer contribution, but the employee deferral limit is shared across every plan you contribute to in the year, so a physician already deferring the maximum through a hospital 403(b) mostly gets the employer side. An S-corp is a payroll-tax question: if hospital wages already exceed the Social Security wage base, the 1099 income only owes the Medicare portion of self-employment tax, so the S-corp saves a few percent of the distribution share and has to beat its own payroll, filing, and state costs. Medicine is a specified service trade or business, so the qualified business income deduction phases out above the income thresholds, and most full-time attendings are above them. A home office deduction is available for the administrative side of a 1099 practice if the space is used exclusively for it, and quarterly estimates keep the 1099 income from producing a penalty in April.

6. Solo 401(k)

A Solo 401(k), also known as an Individual 401(k), is a retirement plan for self-employed individuals with no full-time employees. It allows significantly higher contribution limits than a traditional IRA, plus the ability to take loans from your account and invest in alternative assets.

Potential savings High annual contribution ceiling with catch-up options
Best fit Self-employed individuals with high income
Level intermediate
Typical cost $0 - $300/year depending on provider

7. S-corp election

An S-Corporation election allows business owners to split their income between a reasonable salary (subject to payroll taxes) and distributions (not subject to self-employment tax). This can result in significant savings on the 15.3% self-employment tax.

Potential savings 10-15% of business income
Best fit Self-employed individuals earning $50K+ in profit
Level intermediate
Typical cost $500 - $2,000/year for payroll and additional tax prep

8. Qualified business income deduction

Deducts up to 20% of qualified pass-through business income.

Level intermediate

9. Home office deduction

Deducts the business-use share of your home under the simplified or actual expense method.

Level beginner

10. Estimated tax payments

Times quarterly payments and safe-harbor amounts so you avoid underpayment penalties.

Level beginner

Physician household with a spouse who has time

The largest deductions on this page are rental losses used against physician wages, and a full-time physician almost never qualifies for them alone. Real estate professional status needs more than 750 hours and more than half of working time in real property trades, which a clinical schedule rules out; on a joint return, a spouse who is not working full time elsewhere can meet the test for the household. The short-term rental loophole has a lower bar, material participation without the 750-hour test, and either spouse’s hours count toward it. Cost segregation and bonus depreciation are what make the loss large enough to matter in the first year. Depreciation recapture on sale and the hour log are the price.

11. Real estate professional status

Real Estate Professional Status is an IRS designation that allows qualifying taxpayers to deduct rental real estate losses against their ordinary income without passive activity loss limitations. This can result in significant tax savings for high-income earners who materially participate in their real estate activities.

Potential savings Unlimited passive losses against W-2 income
Best fit Those who spend 750+ hours annually in real estate activities
Level advanced
Typical cost Included in tax preparation, but requires detailed time tracking

12. Short-term rental loophole

A rental whose average period of customer use is seven days or less is not a rental activity under Reg. 1.469-1T(e)(3)(ii)(A). It is treated as a trade or business, so its loss is not passive when you materially participate. That lets the loss offset W-2 income in the same year, without meeting the 750-hour and more-than-half tests for real estate professional status.

Potential savings $30,000 - $100,000+ in tax deductions
Best fit W-2 earners who run the bookings themselves in a short-stay market
Level intermediate
Typical cost Included in tax preparation with STR experience

13. Cost segregation

Cost segregation is a tax strategy that allows real estate investors to accelerate depreciation deductions by reclassifying components of a building into shorter depreciation periods. Instead of depreciating the entire property over 27.5 or 39 years, certain components like carpeting, appliances, and landscaping can be depreciated over 5, 7, or 15 years.

Potential savings $20,000 - $100,000+ in year one
Best fit Properties worth $500K+ purchased or renovated recently
Level advanced
Typical cost $5,000 - $15,000 for a study

14. Bonus depreciation

Bonus depreciation lets you deduct qualifying property in the year it is placed in service instead of spreading it over the recovery period. For 2026 the rate is 100 percent for qualified property acquired after January 19, 2025, made permanent by the 2025 tax law in Section 168(k). Property under a binding contract on or before January 19, 2025 stays on the old phase-down and gets 20 percent in 2026.

Potential savings 100% of qualifying asset cost as a first-year deduction
Best fit Equipment, vehicles, and the short-life components a cost segregation study finds
Level intermediate
Typical cost Included in standard tax preparation

A high-income year: charitable and timing moves

A signing bonus, a partnership buy-in year, or a large Roth conversion pushes taxable income deeper into the top bracket, and that is the year a deduction is worth the most. A donor-advised fund takes the full deduction now and grants the money out over years. Bunching two or three years of gifts into one return clears the standard deduction that a single year’s gifts may not. Appreciated stock given directly, rather than sold and then donated, skips the capital gain as well as earning the deduction.

15. Donor-advised fund

Takes the charitable deduction in the year the account is funded and grants the money out later.

Level intermediate

16. Bunching deductions

Concentrates two years of charitable gifts and other itemized deductions into one year to clear the standard deduction.

Level beginner

Common questions

What is the best tax strategy for physicians?

Fill every pre-tax account the employer offers, including a 457(b) where a nonprofit hospital has one, then add an HSA and a backdoor Roth IRA. That list is fully under your control and needs no business. Moonlighting or locums income adds a solo 401(k) and an S-corp analysis. Rental losses against physician wages, the largest numbers, usually need a spouse who can meet the participation tests.

Do physicians qualify for the QBI deduction?

Only on self-employment or practice income, and only when taxable income is below the thresholds. Medicine is a specified service trade or business, so the qualified business income deduction phases out above the income thresholds and then disappears. An employed physician with no 1099 income has no qualified business income at all.

Can a physician with a full-time hospital job use the short-term rental loophole?

Yes, if you or your spouse materially participate in the rental and the average guest stay is seven days or less. The loophole does not need the 750-hour real estate professional test, which is what makes it reachable on a clinical schedule. Material participation still has its own hour requirements, usually 100 hours and more than anyone else, and it needs a contemporaneous log.

Should a moonlighting physician form an S-corp?

Only after modeling it. If hospital wages already exceed the Social Security wage base, the 1099 income owes only the Medicare portion of self-employment tax, so the S-corp saves a few percent of the distribution share and must cover its own payroll, filing, and state costs. It usually starts to make sense when the 1099 income is large and steady, not in the first year of occasional shifts.

Is a 457(b) separate from a 403(b) limit?

Yes. A 457(b) has its own deferral limit, separate from the shared 401(k) and 403(b) limit, so a physician at a nonprofit hospital that offers both can defer into both in the same year. The caveat is that a non-governmental 457(b) stays an unsecured asset of the employer until it is paid out, and the distribution schedule is set when you leave, so read the plan document before relying on it.
