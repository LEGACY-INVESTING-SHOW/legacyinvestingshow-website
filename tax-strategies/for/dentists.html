Tax strategies for dentists

For an associate, the best tax strategy is the account list: the practice 401(k), an HSA, a backdoor Roth, and a solo 401(k) if the pay is 1099. For a practice owner, the two largest levers are a reasonable S-corp salary paired with a cash balance or defined benefit plan, and depreciation on equipment and the building. At exit, how the sale is structured decides the tax more than any deduction in the years before it.

Dentistry is a specified service business with heavy equipment, often a building, and usually a sale at the end. That makes the tax picture different from a physician’s: less can be done inside payroll and far more inside the practice entity. The moves below follow the career, from associate to owner to seller.

How the moves are grouped

Grouped by what each one asks of you, not by the size of the deduction. Work down from the top of the first group. Some can be put in place during the year. Some need an account opened before money moves. Some only work if the documentation exists before the deduction is claimed.

Associate dentist (W-2 or 1099)

A W-2 associate has the same list as any employee: fill the practice’s 401(k) if there is one, an HSA if the health plan qualifies, and a backdoor Roth IRA above the income limits. A 1099 associate is self-employed, which opens a solo 401(k) with both employee and employer contributions, plus quarterly estimated payments and the home office and vehicle deductions for the administrative side of the work. The S-corp question at this stage depends on the size of the 1099 income and the state; in the first year it is often premature.

1. Health savings account

A Health Savings Account (HSA) offers a triple tax advantage: contributions are tax-deductible, growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free. Many investors use HSAs as stealth retirement accounts by paying current medical expenses out-of-pocket and letting the HSA grow.

Potential savings Triple tax benefit with annual contribution limits
Best fit Healthy individuals with high-deductible health plans
Level beginner
Typical cost Free with most providers

2. Backdoor Roth IRA

Reaches a Roth IRA through a non-deductible contribution and conversion when income exceeds the Roth limits.

Level intermediate

3. Solo 401(k)

A Solo 401(k), also known as an Individual 401(k), is a retirement plan for self-employed individuals with no full-time employees. It allows significantly higher contribution limits than a traditional IRA, plus the ability to take loans from your account and invest in alternative assets.

Potential savings High annual contribution ceiling with catch-up options
Best fit Self-employed individuals with high income
Level intermediate
Typical cost $0 - $300/year depending on provider

4. Estimated tax payments

Times quarterly payments and safe-harbor amounts so you avoid underpayment penalties.

Level beginner

Practice owner

Once you own the practice, the entity and the retirement plan do most of the work. An S-corp splits pay into a reasonable salary and distributions, and reasonable for a producing dentist is benchmarked against what an associate would be paid for the same clinical work, so the distribution share is smaller than owners hope. A cash balance or defined benefit plan layered on top of the 401(k) allows the largest deductible contributions available to a stable, high-income owner, at the cost of required contributions for eligible staff. That staff point is also the catch with a solo 401(k): once the practice has employees, it stops being solo and the plan has to cover them. Section 179 and bonus depreciation cover chairs, imaging, milling units, and other equipment in the year it is placed in service. The Augusta rule rents your home to the practice for up to 14 days of real meetings. Hiring your children for real work moves income into their bracket. The qualified business income deduction phases out for specified service businesses above the income thresholds, so most owners cannot count on it, and a pass-through entity tax election can rescue part of the state tax deduction where the state offers one.

5. S-corp election

An S-Corporation election allows business owners to split their income between a reasonable salary (subject to payroll taxes) and distributions (not subject to self-employment tax). This can result in significant savings on the 15.3% self-employment tax.

Potential savings 10-15% of business income
Best fit Self-employed individuals earning $50K+ in profit
Level intermediate
Typical cost $500 - $2,000/year for payroll and additional tax prep

6. Section 179 expensing

Section 179 allows businesses to deduct the full purchase price of qualifying equipment and software purchased during the tax year, rather than depreciating it over time. The deduction limit and phase-out threshold are updated by the IRS each year.

Potential savings Up to $1,160,000 immediate deduction
Best fit Businesses purchasing equipment, vehicles, or software
Level beginner
Typical cost Included in standard tax preparation

7. Bonus depreciation

Bonus depreciation lets you deduct qualifying property in the year it is placed in service instead of spreading it over the recovery period. For 2026 the rate is 100 percent for qualified property acquired after January 19, 2025, made permanent by the 2025 tax law in Section 168(k). Property under a binding contract on or before January 19, 2025 stays on the old phase-down and gets 20 percent in 2026.

Potential savings 100% of qualifying asset cost as a first-year deduction
Best fit Equipment, vehicles, and the short-life components a cost segregation study finds
Level intermediate
Typical cost Included in standard tax preparation

8. Augusta rule

The Augusta Rule allows homeowners to rent their home for up to 14 days per year without reporting the rental income. Business owners can leverage this by having their business rent their personal residence for meetings, retreats, or events at fair market rates.

Potential savings $5,000 - $30,000 tax-free income
Best fit Business owners who can legitimately use their home for business purposes
Level intermediate
Typical cost May need appraisal for fair market rent ($200-500)

9. Hiring your children

Puts family members on payroll for real work, moving income into a lower bracket.

Level intermediate

10. Qualified business income deduction

Deducts up to 20% of qualified pass-through business income.

Level intermediate

11. Pass-through entity tax

Pays state tax at the entity level so the deduction survives the federal SALT cap.

Level intermediate

Practice real estate

Many owners buy the building. Holding it in a separate entity and leasing it to the practice is the standard way to keep the building apart from the practice’s liabilities, and it lets the building be sold or kept independently of the practice at exit. Cost segregation on a dental build-out is productive because so much of the cost sits in finishes, plumbing, and equipment-specific systems with short recovery lives. The catch is the self-rental rule: net rental income from a property leased to a business in which you materially participate is treated as non-passive, so it cannot absorb passive losses from other rentals, while a net loss from the building stays passive. The depreciation still lowers the rent that is taxed; it does not create a loss you can use against practice income.

12. Cost segregation

Cost segregation is a tax strategy that allows real estate investors to accelerate depreciation deductions by reclassifying components of a building into shorter depreciation periods. Instead of depreciating the entire property over 27.5 or 39 years, certain components like carpeting, appliances, and landscaping can be depreciated over 5, 7, or 15 years.

Potential savings $20,000 - $100,000+ in year one
Best fit Properties worth $500K+ purchased or renovated recently
Level advanced
Typical cost $5,000 - $15,000 for a study

13. Bonus depreciation

Bonus depreciation lets you deduct qualifying property in the year it is placed in service instead of spreading it over the recovery period. For 2026 the rate is 100 percent for qualified property acquired after January 19, 2025, made permanent by the 2025 tax law in Section 168(k). Property under a binding contract on or before January 19, 2025 stays on the old phase-down and gets 20 percent in 2026.

Potential savings 100% of qualifying asset cost as a first-year deduction
Best fit Equipment, vehicles, and the short-life components a cost segregation study finds
Level intermediate
Typical cost Included in standard tax preparation

14. Rental property depreciation

Deducts a rental building over 27.5 years and tracks the basis that matters at sale.

Level beginner

Selling the practice

The exit is usually the largest taxable event of a dental career, and the structure decides the rate. Purchase price allocation splits the sale among goodwill, equipment, and covenants, and each piece is taxed differently: goodwill at capital gains rates, equipment depreciation at ordinary recapture rates. An installment sale spreads the gain across the years payments are received, which can keep part of it under the top bracket and the net investment income tax threshold, at the cost of collection risk on the buyer. A charitable remainder trust converts part of an appreciated practice or building into lifetime income with a current deduction. If the building is sold separately, a 1031 exchange can defer that gain into another property.

15. Installment sale

Spreads the gain on a sale across the years the payments are actually received.

Level intermediate

16. Charitable remainder trust

Converts an appreciated asset into lifetime income with a current deduction and deferred gain.

Level advanced

17. 1031 exchange

A 1031 exchange, named after Section 1031 of the IRS code, allows real estate investors to defer paying capital gains taxes when they sell an investment property and reinvest the proceeds into another 'like-kind' property. This powerful strategy lets investors grow their portfolio tax-free as long as they continue exchanging properties.

Potential savings 15-20% capital gains tax deferral
Best fit Investors looking to upgrade or diversify their real estate portfolio
Level advanced
Typical cost $1,000 - $3,000 for qualified intermediary

Common questions

What is the best tax strategy for dentists?

It depends on the stage. An associate should fill the practice 401(k), an HSA, and a backdoor Roth, and open a solo 401(k) if paid on a 1099. A practice owner gets the most from an S-corp with a defensible salary, a cash balance plan stacked on the 401(k), and Section 179 or bonus depreciation on equipment. An owner who also holds the building adds cost segregation. At exit, the sale structure matters more than any single deduction.

Do dentists qualify for QBI?

Sometimes. Dentistry is a specified service trade or business, so the qualified business income deduction is full below the income thresholds, phases out through a range above them, and is gone past it. An associate with modest 1099 income may qualify; a producing owner usually does not. Because the test is on taxable income, a large retirement plan contribution can pull some owners back under the thresholds in a given year.

How much salary does a dentist S-corp have to pay?

Enough to be reasonable for the clinical work you do, which is benchmarked against what an associate would be paid to produce the same dentistry. A producing owner therefore has a high reasonable salary and a smaller distribution share than a non-producing owner would. The saving is the payroll tax on distributions, and above the Social Security wage base only the Medicare portion of that tax remains.

Can a dentist deduct the practice building?

Through depreciation, and often faster through a cost segregation study, because a dental build-out is heavy in short-life components. If you own the building in a separate entity and lease it to your practice, the self-rental rule treats the net rental income as non-passive, so the building’s depreciation lowers the taxed rent but a net loss cannot offset practice income or absorb other passive losses.

Is a cash balance plan worth it for a dental practice?

It is the largest deductible contribution available to a stable, high-income owner, and it stacks on top of a 401(k). The trade is that it is a defined benefit plan: contributions are required each year, eligible staff must be covered, and it needs an actuary. It fits an owner with several years of consistent profit who wants to shelter a large amount before an exit, and it does not fit a practice with volatile income.
