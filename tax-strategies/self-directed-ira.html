What is a Self-Directed IRA? Quick Overview

A stunning 87% of retirement accounts remain concentrated in traditional stocks and bonds, missing real estate and alternative investments that drive wealth for high-net-worth individuals. Self-Directed IRAs (SDIRAs) break this mold by allowing you to invest retirement funds in a broader spectrum of assets—real estate, private businesses, precious metals, cryptocurrency, and more—while maintaining tax-advantaged growth.

Bottom Line: A Self-Directed IRA lets you control investment decisions (within IRS rules) and invest in assets beyond the typical brokerage offerings, potentially multiplying your retirement wealth through properties generating rental income, private equity stakes, or alternative assets while tax-deferred or tax-free growth compounds your returns.

Quick Benefits Summary:

  • Invest in income-producing real estate inside tax-sheltered account
  • Tax-deferred or tax-free growth on alternative investments
  • Diversify retirement beyond traditional stocks and bonds
  • Participate in private deals and business investments
  • Hold precious metals and cryptocurrency (with right custodian)
  • Leverage tax code sections (IRC 408, 4975) for wealth building

Comprehensive Definition: How Self-Directed IRAs Work

A Self-Directed IRA is a retirement account that gives you—not a financial institution—control over investment decisions. Unlike traditional IRAs managed by brokerage firms offering limited investment menus, an SDIRA lets you direct your retirement custodian to purchase almost any asset allowed by IRS regulations.

How the Structure Works

You establish the account with an SDIRA custodian (specialized provider) who holds legal title to assets but executes only your investment instructions. The custodian acts as a passive administrator—they maintain records, file tax forms, and ensure prohibited transaction compliance, but you make all investment decisions. This distinction is crucial: you direct, the custodian executes and safeguards.

Legal Framework & IRS Authority

Self-Directed IRAs operate under IRC Section 408 (authorizing IRA establishments), IRC Section 4975 (defining prohibited transactions), and Treasury Regulations Section 1.408-1. The IRS explicitly permits IRAs to hold alternative investments, provided they're not "collectibles" or disqualified from code sections. Key legislative milestones include the Taxpayer Relief Act of 1997 (expanding alternative investment eligibility) and consistent IRS guidance clarifying permissible investments like real estate, notes, and precious metals.

Historical Context

SDIRAs emerged in the 1970s following IRA creation but remained niche until the 2000s when real estate investors discovered their power. The 2008 financial crisis accelerated adoption as investors sought alternatives to stock-heavy retirement accounts. Today, estimated $500 billion to $1 trillion in SDIRA assets fund everything from apartment complexes to private businesses.

Traditional vs. Self-Directed vs. Roth Mechanics

All three are IRAs at their core, distinguished by tax treatment and flexibility. Traditional SDIRAs allow tax-deductible contributions with deferred taxation on growth. Roth SDIRAs require after-tax contributions but generate completely tax-free growth and withdrawals. Self-Directed simply refers to account control, applicable to both Traditional and Roth structures. The investment range differs significantly: standard IRAs restrict you to brokerage-house investments, while SDIRAs unlock alternative asset classes.

Who Benefits Most from Self-Directed IRAs?

Persona 1: Real Estate Investor (Marcus, 42)

Situation: Marcus generates $80,000 annually in rental income from properties he personally owns. He's maxing standard investment accounts but wants to shelter more income and diversify into larger multifamily deals requiring scale beyond his personal capacity.

SDIRA Benefit: By rolling his existing $150,000 IRA into a Self-Directed SDIRA, Marcus co-invests with partners in a 24-unit apartment complex, using 1031-exchange proceeds. The SDIRA purchases $350,000 of equity (with debt-financed portion triggering UBIT), and net rental income flows tax-deferred through the account. Over 10 years, he accumulates $520,000 in appreciation without annual income tax drag.

Persona 2: Business Owner with Private Equity Ambitions (Jennifer, 38)

Situation: Jennifer built a software consulting business generating $250,000+ profit annually. Her business partner offered her a 5% stake in a tech startup (non-traded). She wants to invest $75,000 but doesn't want that future appreciation taxed annually as her business grows.

SDIRA Benefit: Jennifer combines a Solo 401(k) (higher contribution limits) with Self-Directed capabilities, purchasing $75,000 of startup equity inside the account. If the startup exits at 10x valuation ($750,000), she realizes $525,000 of tax-free or tax-deferred appreciation, versus $130,000+ in taxes if held personally.

Persona 3: Precious Metals Enthusiast (Robert, 55)

Situation: Robert believes gold and silver will appreciate significantly amid currency concerns. He has $200,000 to invest but currently holds metals in personal vaults, which generates no tax efficiency and ties up capital subject to capital gains taxation.

SDIRA Benefit: Moving $200,000 of IRS-approved bullion into an SDIRA vault allows tax-deferred growth. If gold rises 40% ($280,000 value), the appreciation remains sheltered. Upon retirement, distributions can be taken in kind (physical metals) or sales, with tax treatment determined at that time.

Persona 4: Cryptocurrency Allocator (Alex, 35)

Situation: Alex wants to allocate 10% of retirement savings ($50,000) to Bitcoin and Ethereum but is wary of holding crypto personally (security, tax reporting complexity). He seeks a regulated, sheltered environment.

SDIRA Benefit: Through a specialized custodian enabling cryptocurrency, Alex purchases Bitcoin and Ethereum inside the account. If his $50,000 grows to $200,000, he's sheltered from annual capital gains tax. Distributions maintain long-term capital gain treatment (if held >1 year before distribution).

Persona 5: Tax-Lien Investor (Sharon, 48)

Situation: Sharon invests in tax liens (county-issued debt instruments paying 8-15% returns). She has been doing this in personal accounts and reinvesting returns, generating $30,000-$40,000 in annual taxable income.

SDIRA Benefit: Moving $125,000 into an SDIRA for tax lien acquisition, Sharon's $35,000 annual returns compound tax-deferred. Over 20 years, tax deferral saves roughly $200,000 in avoided annual tax drag.

Step-by-Step Implementation: Getting Started with Your SDIRA

Step 1: Determine Your Account Type (Days 1-3)

Action: Decide between Traditional SDIRA (tax-deductible contributions, taxable distributions), Roth SDIRA (after-tax contributions, tax-free growth), or SEP SDIRA (if self-employed). Consult your tax advisor on which optimizes your situation.

Timeline: 1-3 days of research and professional consultation

Documents Needed: None yet—this is planning stage

Pro Tip: If you're unsure, many investors open Traditional SDIRA first, then execute Roth conversions later when income dips.

Step 2: Select an SDIRA Custodian (Days 3-10)

Action: Research specialized custodians. Options include Directed Individual Account (DIA), uDirect, Alto IRA, Rocket Dollar, and Equity Trust. Evaluate based on: (1) asset types supported (real estate, crypto, precious metals?), (2) fee structure (annual maintenance, transaction fees, storage fees), (3) customer service reputation, (4) IRS audit defense track record.

Timeline: 1-2 weeks of evaluation; some custodians onboard within days

Documents Needed: Photo ID, proof of address, Social Security number (standard account opening)

Pitfall: Don't choose based on lowest fees alone—poor compliance support costs far more if you face IRS issues

Pro Tip: Interview custodians about prohibited transaction experience; ask how they handle loan documentation for real estate purchases

Step 3: Fund Your SDIRA (Days 10-30)

Action: Execute one of three funding methods: (A) Direct contribution (up to $7,000 in 2024, $8,000 if age 50+), (B) Rollover (IRA-to-SDIRA direct trustee transfer), or (C) 401(k) rollover (if leaving employment or plan permits in-service rollover). For rollovers, request custodian-to-custodian transfer to avoid 60-day distribution rules.

Timeline: Direct contribution instant; rollover 2-4 weeks depending on prior custodian processing

Documents Needed: Bank wire authorization, rollover request forms, prior custodian account statements

Pitfall: Indirect rollovers trigger 20% withholding tax; you must personally contribute the withheld amount within 60 days or face penalties

Pro Tip: Always request direct (trustee-to-trustee) rollovers in writing to avoid complications

Step 4: Identify Your Investment (Days 20-60)

Action: Research and identify specific investments—a rental property, private business stake, precious metals, tax liens, or cryptocurrency. For real estate, conduct due diligence: property inspection, title search, comparable market analysis, tenant screening (if applicable). For private equity, review business plans and financial statements. For cryptocurrency, evaluate exchange security and custodial insurance.

Timeline: Highly variable; real estate 30-90 days; private deals 20-60 days; metals/crypto 5-15 days

Documents Needed: Purchase agreements, property appraisals, title reports, business documents, investment memoranda

Pitfall: Moving too fast without proper due diligence; rushing into investments pressures poor decisions

Pro Tip: Have legal and financial advisors review deals BEFORE committing; prohibited transaction violations are not reversible

Step 5: Ensure Loan Documentation Compliance (If Applicable) (Days 40-75)

Action: If purchasing real estate with leverage, execute a non-recourse loan (the SDIRA is liable, you are not). Work with your custodian to coordinate loan documentation. The lender, SDIRA trustee, and you must all be parties to confirm the SDIRA (not you personally) is the borrower.

Timeline: 2-4 weeks; lenders experienced with SDIRAs move faster

Documents Needed: Promissory note, deed of trust/mortgage, title insurance, appraisal, FNMA loan application

Pitfall: Recourse loans (where you're personally liable) disqualify non-recourse tax benefits and trigger adverse UBIT/UDFI consequences

Pro Tip: Use SDIRA-experienced lenders (SDIRA.com has referrals); they know exactly how to structure documents correctly

Step 6: Execute Investment Purchase (Days 60-90)

Action: Wire funds from SDIRA custodian to close on the investment. For real estate, coordinate with title company to ensure deed is issued in SDIRA trustee's name (e.g., "Directed Individual Account, FBO [Your Name], Custodian"). For precious metals, arrange shipment to approved depository. For cryptocurrency, fund exchange account designated as SDIRA-owned.

Timeline: 1-5 days for transfer and closing

Documents Needed: Closing statements, deeds, purchase confirmations, receipt documentation

Pitfall: Improper title registration (e.g., deed in your personal name) constitutes prohibited transaction

Pro Tip: Request custodian to send funds directly to closing agent; avoid personal control of funds

Step 7: Maintain Records & Monitor Compliance (Ongoing)

Action: Keep comprehensive records: purchase agreements, receipts, bank statements, rental agreements, operating expenses, loan statements. File annual Form 5498 (custodian sends), and report SDIRA distributions on Form 1099-R. Review annually for disqualified person transactions or prohibited dealings.

Timeline: Ongoing; annual IRS filing deadline is tax return due date

Documents Needed: All investment documents, tax statements, audit support materials

Pitfall: Poor documentation exposes you to IRS challenges; maintain records for 7+ years

Pro Tip: Use digital management (shared folder with accountant); sync with custodian for accuracy

Step 8: Review & Rebalance Annually (Yearly)

Action: Work with tax advisor each year to review investment performance, ensure continued compliance, and assess rebalancing needs. Execute 1031 exchanges if real estate appreciation warrants upgrading to better properties.

Timeline: Annual tax planning session

Documents Needed: Year-end statements, investment valuations, tax estimates

Pro Tip: Real estate can be exchanged tax-deferred; use 1031 exchanges to upgrade properties without triggering gains

Expert Strategies: Advanced SDIRA Tactics

Strategy 1: 1031 Exchange Chain Within SDIRA

How It Works: Sell a rental property held inside your SDIRA and immediately reinvest proceeds into a like-kind property (real estate for real estate) without triggering capital gains. This preserves the tax-deferred growth and allows upgrading to higher-appreciation properties.

Implementation Steps:

  1. Contact a qualified intermediary who specializes in SDIRA 1031 exchanges (e.g., Inland Diversified)
  2. Sell property; proceeds go to qualified intermediary (never touch them personally)
  3. Within 45 days, identify replacement properties (up to 3 properties, or unlimited if 95% of value rule met)
  4. Close on replacement property within 180 days using SDIRA custodian funds
  5. Custodian holds title to new property; IRA continues tax-deferred growth

Tax Impact: Zero federal capital gains tax on property sale; 100% of proceeds reinvested for growth. Executed correctly, a $500,000 property sold for $750,000 appreciation stays sheltered—no $112,500 capital gains tax.

Pro Tip: Time 1031 exchanges when properties appreciate significantly; the gains become realized but still sheltered. Do NOT purchase the replacement property in your personal name; ensure custodian acquires title.

Strategy 2: Leverage Non-Recourse Financing for Real Estate Appreciation

How It Works: Use borrowed funds (non-recourse only) to amplify real estate purchases within your SDIRA. The SDIRA puts down 20-30%, borrows 70-80% from a lender specializing in SDIRA loans. Appreciation on the entire property value accrues to the SDIRA.

Implementation Steps:

  1. Identify target property; obtain appraisal and inspection
  2. Contact SDIRA-friendly lender (e.g., SDIRA-specific banks, hard money lenders)
  3. Provide lender with SDIRA custodian information and loan structure requirements
  4. Lender underwrites non-recourse loan to SDIRA (you sign nothing personally)
  5. Custodian closes loan and purchase; title held in SDIRA trustee's name
  6. SDIRA pays loan payments from rental income or reserves

Tax Impact: Non-recourse loans are not treated as SDIRA distributions. Interest is deductible against rental income. Debt-financed property portion generates Unrelated Debt-Financed Income (UDFI) subject to UBIT, but non-borrowed portion and appreciation remain sheltered. UBIT liability typically minimal for real estate.

Critical UBIT Concern: Debt-financed property UBIT is a trap. If SDIRA borrows $300,000 on a $400,000 property (75% debt ratio), approximately 75% of annual net income is UDFI and subject to UBIT at trust tax rates (~37% top). However, mortgage principal paydown is not income; only net rental income is taxed. Many SDIRAs successfully use leverage and pay minimal UBIT.

Pro Tip: Calculate UDFI/UBIT before financing. Work with custodian to estimate tax. High-leverage deals may have UBIT exceeding benefits.

Strategy 3: Roth Conversion with SDIRA for Tax-Free Growth

How It Works: Convert Traditional SDIRA funds to Roth SDIRA (paying taxes upfront), then invest in high-growth assets. All future appreciation is permanently tax-free—ideal for long-term real estate or cryptocurrency bets.

Implementation Steps:

  1. Open Roth SDIRA with same custodian or rollover-ready custodian
  2. Initiate conversion from Traditional SDIRA to Roth SDIRA
  3. Pay federal and state income tax on amount converted (can use non-SDIRA funds)
  4. Invest Roth SDIRA in high-growth assets (real estate expected to appreciate 5%+ annually, emerging private businesses, cryptocurrency)
  5. All growth compounds tax-free; distributions after age 59½ (5-year rule satisfied) are entirely tax-free

Tax Impact: Conversion year generates one-time tax; long-term growth elimination is massive. Example: Convert $100,000 Traditional SDIRA to Roth (pay $30,000 tax); invest in property that grows to $300,000 over 20 years. Traditional SDIRA would owe ~$70,000 in taxes on distribution; Roth owes $0.

Timing Consideration: Execute conversions in low-income years (retirement, sabbatical, business downturn) to minimize tax. Pro Tip: Backdoor Roth with SDIRA assets if pro-rata rule is triggered.

Strategy 4: Private Promissory Notes & Seller Financing

How It Works: Loan money from your SDIRA to a related party (not disqualified, such as a non-spousal cousin or adult child's business) via a formal promissory note, capturing interest payments and principal repayment as SDIRA income.

Implementation Steps:

  1. Identify borrower (must not be disqualified person—spouse, parent, child, entity you control)
  2. Establish loan terms: principal amount, interest rate (minimum Applicable Federal Rate, ~5-6%), repayment schedule, default provisions
  3. Execute formal promissory note with borrower (not disqualified person, perhaps a business partner or cousin)
  4. SDIRA custodian approves note terms and disburses funds
  5. Borrower makes monthly/quarterly payments to SDIRA custodian
  6. SDIRA accumulates interest income tax-deferred

Tax Impact: Interest income earned is tax-deferred if Traditional SDIRA, tax-free if Roth SDIRA. Avoid disqualified persons at all costs—loaning to yourself or spouse triggers prohibited transaction and plan disqualification.

Pro Tip: Document everything. Interest must be at or above IRS Applicable Federal Rate (AFR) to avoid imputed interest complications. Rates: Long-term (9+ years) ~5%, Mid-term (3-9 years) ~5%, Short-term (<3 years) ~5%. Check current AFR rates monthly.

Strategy 5: Checkbook Control LLC for Real-Time Investment Decisions

How It Works: Create a Limited Liability Company owned by your SDIRA. The LLC has a checking account and debit card. You (as LLC manager, not owner) can write checks and invest immediately without waiting for custodian approval on each transaction.

Implementation Steps:

  1. Establish single-member LLC in any state (Delaware is common for privacy)
  2. Fund LLC by having SDIRA custodian transfer funds to LLC bank account
  3. You become manager; SDIRA retains ownership (100% LLC equity held by SDIRA)
  4. Open LLC business checking account; obtain debit card
  5. As manager, execute investments, sign contracts, make purchases—all in LLC name
  6. LLC owns investments on behalf of SDIRA

Tax Impact: LLC is disregarded for tax purposes (all income/gains report on SDIRA return). Flexibility is massive—you can write checks to contractors, purchase equipment, close on real estate without custodian intermediation on every transaction.

Critical Caveat: Checkbook control does NOT exempt you from prohibited transaction rules. You can still personally benefit; you can still transact with disqualified persons; you can still violate UBIT. Many SDIRA violations occur with checkbook control because people assume control equals freedom. It does not. Work with SDIRA-experienced attorney to structure LLC and document decision-making.

Pro Tip: Custodians often charge higher annual fees for checkbook control ($500-$1,000 vs. $200-$300 standard); ensure benefits justify costs.

Common Mistakes: How to Avoid Costly SDIRA Errors

Mistake 1: Prohibited Transactions with Family

The Error: Marcus wants to help his son open a business. He writes a check from his SDIRA's checkbook LLC directly to his son's business. The son later defaults. Marcus assumes it's fine because he's "helping family."

IRS Reality: Loan to son (disqualified person child) is prohibited transaction. SDIRA disqualified; Marcus is deemed to have received entire account balance; he owes income tax on full SDIRA value + 15% penalty tax. Additionally, any income previously earned in SDIRA is retroactively taxable.

Recovery Strategy: None. Prohibited transactions are not reversible. Marcus could have sold his SDIRA and invested personally, but once the transaction occurs, the damage is permanent. He must recognize income and penalties in that tax year.

Prevention: Before any transaction, confirm counterparty is NOT disqualified. Disqualified = you, spouse, children, grandchildren, parents, grandparents, any company you control >50%, or any disqualified entity. When in doubt, use unrelated parties.

Mistake 2: Taking Personal Benefit from SDIRA Real Estate

The Error: Jennifer purchases a vacation cabin via her SDIRA. She and her family use it 3 weeks per year; the remainder is rented to third parties (8 weeks/year actual rental, 17 weeks personal use).

IRS Reality: Personal use of SDIRA-owned real estate is prohibited transaction. SDIRA disqualified; Jennifer must recognize entire account balance as distribution + 15% penalty. Additionally, 28 years of tax deferral is now taxed in one year.

Recovery Strategy: Very limited. Jennifer could argue no personal use occurred (if documentation proves it), but vacation cabin with family visits fails scrutiny. If discovered during audit, penalties apply retroactively.

Prevention: SDIRA real estate must be purely investment property. No personal use, no renovation by you (must hire third-party contractors), no services provided by you, no decision-making benefit to you personally. Treat SDIRA properties as if you'll never set foot in them.

Mistake 3: Self-Dealing & Personal Services

The Error: Robert owns an SDIRA. He hires his own handyman business to renovate a rental property held in the SDIRA, paying $50,000 for labor and materials. He believes it's fine because he owns both entities.

IRS Reality: Self-dealing with entity you control is prohibited transaction. SDIRA disqualified; Robert must report entire account balance as income + 15% penalty. The IRS may also challenge whether valuation was fair-market (self-dealing often leads to inflated prices).

Recovery Strategy: Limited. Robert could petition for IRS relief citing reasonable cause, but self-dealing is intentional and rarely granted relief. Retroactive taxation and penalties apply.

Prevention: Never use services from yourself, your business, or entities you control. Hire unrelated third parties. Document all hiring as fair-market-value competitive bids. Maintain records proving arms-length transactions.

Mistake 4: Failure to File Form 5498-SA or Misreporting UBIT

The Error: Sarah purchases a debt-financed rental property in her SDIRA using a non-recourse loan. Her custodian fails to file Form 5498-SA properly; the UBIT liability is not calculated. Sarah doesn't file UBIT tax returns (Form 990-T) because she's unaware.

IRS Reality: UBIT liability is Sarah's responsibility even if custodian errs. IRS may assess UBIT + interest + penalties. If Sarah is audited on her personal return, SDIRA inconsistencies may trigger examination. Failure to file Form 990-T (UBIT return) is separate violation.

Recovery Strategy: File amended UBIT returns immediately upon discovery. Work with CPA experienced in SDIRA UBIT to determine accurate liability. Reasonable cause relief may apply if custodian error is documented.

Prevention: (1) Work with custodian experienced in UBIT calculations. (2) Engage CPA each year to review SDIRA investments and assess UBIT. (3) File Form 990-T proactively for any debt-financed property. (4) Calculate UDFI before leveraging property to understand UBIT exposure upfront.

Mistake 5: Mixing Personal and SDIRA Funds During Investment

The Error: David is purchasing real estate via SDIRA. He contributes $80,000 to SDIRA, but the property costs $200,000. He deposits $120,000 of personal funds into SDIRA temporarily to complete the purchase, intending to withdraw them after closing.

IRS Reality: SDIRA receives co-ownership benefit (property titled partly to SDIRA, partly to David personally). IRS treats this as prohibited transaction OR recharacterizes the property ownership. Additionally, David's withdrawals from SDIRA are subject to income tax + 10% penalty if he's under 59½.

Recovery Strategy: Restructure transaction. SDIRA should take majority ownership; David invests personally in separate deal. This way, no prohibited transaction occurs, and each account maintains separate assets.

Prevention: SDIRA funds purchase SDIRA-held assets exclusively. Personal funds purchase personal assets. If co-investment is desired, structure as SDIRA % + personal %, with separate title documentation and financing for each portion. Consult SDIRA-experienced attorney.

Mistake 6: Inadequate Documentation for Audit Defense

The Error: Tom purchases a $500,000 property via SDIRA. He maintains minimal records—no appraisal, no due diligence documentation, no investment thesis. IRS audits SDIRA 5 years later and asks how he valued the property.

IRS Reality: Without documentation, IRS questions valuation, method of selection, and investment legitimacy. Tom cannot defend against IRS argument that the property was overvalued or purchased improperly. SDIRA faces potential disqualification if IRS believes prohibited transaction or self-dealing occurred.

Recovery Strategy: Reconstruct documentation retroactively (difficult, unreliable). Work with auditor and provide any available evidence. If no evidence exists, IRS determination often stands.

Prevention: Maintain comprehensive records: (1) Investment thesis and due diligence for each purchase, (2) Independent appraisals, (3) Competitive bids and vendor selection documentation, (4) Correspondence with custodian approving investments, (5) Title documents, (6) Annual valuations for financial statements, (7) Loan documents (if any), (8) All receipts and contracts. Store digitally and back up.

When to Choose Self-Directed IRA vs. Solo 401(k)?

Choose SDIRA if:

  • You contribute <$50,000 annually (SDIRA within limits)
  • Your focus is real estate or alternative investments you've identified
  • You want simplicity over higher contribution limits
  • You don't have substantial self-employment income
  • Annual cost matters; SDIRAs cheaper than Solo 401(k)s

Choose Solo 401(k) if:

  • You're self-employed earning $100,000+ net self-employment income
  • You want to maximize retirement contributions (up to $69,000 in 2024)
  • UBIT/UDFI concerns exist; Solo 401(k)s have better tax treatment for leveraged real estate
  • You want additional borrowing power (Solo 401(k) loans available; SDIRA loans not)
  • You need greater administrative control and checkbook access

Frequently Asked Questions: Detailed Answers

Self-Directed IRAs permit a vast array of investments: real estate (residential, commercial, raw land), private businesses (shares, partnerships), precious metals (IRS-approved gold, silver, platinum, palladium bullion), cryptocurrency (Bitcoin, Ethereum, etc.), tax liens, promissory notes, equipment leasing, operating businesses (with restrictions), and more. Prohibited investments include life insurance, collectible artwork and coins (except IRS-approved bullion), S-corporation stock, and transactions with disqualified persons. The key principle: if it's not explicitly prohibited, it's generally allowed.

Yes, absolutely. You can purchase residential rentals, commercial properties, multifamily buildings, and raw land inside your SDIRA. However, strict rules apply: (1) All expenses (mortgage, taxes, insurance, repairs, maintenance) must be paid by the SDIRA, never personally. (2) All income (rent, deposits, proceeds) flows to the SDIRA, not to you personally. (3) You cannot personally use the property (no family vacations, no storing personal items). (4) You cannot perform repairs or improvements yourself; only hire third-party contractors. (5) You cannot personally benefit in any way. Any personal benefit violates prohibited transaction rules and disqualifies the entire account.

Prohibited transactions (defined in IRC Section 4975) are transactions between the SDIRA and disqualified persons, or uses of SDIRA assets for personal benefit. Specific prohibitions: (1) Transacting with disqualified persons (yourself, spouse, parents, children, grandchildren, ancestors, any entity you control >50%, or fiduciaries of the plan). (2) Using SDIRA assets for personal benefit (personal loans, living arrangements, entertainment). (3) Receiving compensation for managing SDIRA investments (unless a third-party professional is hired). (4) Self-dealing (selling your personal property to the SDIRA at marked-up price; purchasing SDIRA property at discount). (5) Borrowing from the SDIRA (except Non-Recourse SDIRA loans for real estate). Violation consequences: entire SDIRA is deemed distributed; you owe income tax on full account value + 15% penalty tax. Penalties are not reversible.

Custodian fees vary by provider and asset complexity. Standard SDIRA fees: $200-$400 annually. Specialized assets (real estate, cryptocurrency, private equity): $400-$800 annually. Checkbook control LLCs: $500-$1,200 annually (due to increased administrative complexity). Additional fees may include: transaction fees ($50-$150 per transaction), account setup ($0-$200), loan arrangement fees ($500-$2,000 for non-recourse real estate loans), precious metals storage ($100-$400 annually), cryptocurrency custody insurance ($0-$200 annually). Despite costs, tax savings typically exceed fees by multiples (example: $100,000 property generating $16,000 annual income saves ~$5,000+ in annual taxes, more than offsetting $300-$500 custodian fee).

Disqualified persons are defined in IRC Section 4975(e)(2) and include: (1) The IRA owner (you). (2) Your spouse. (3) Your lineal descendants (children, grandchildren, great-grandchildren). (4) Your ancestors (parents, grandparents, great-grandparents). (5) Any spouse of descendant or ancestor (in-laws via children/parents). (6) Any entity in which you hold >50% interest (your business, partnership, corporation). (7) Any fiduciary of the plan (custodian, advisor). Notably, siblings, cousins, and more distant relatives are NOT disqualified. This permits loans or investments with extended family (sibling, cousin business) as long as you don't control the entity. Transactions with disqualified persons trigger prohibited transaction status immediately; IRA disqualification is automatic and irrevocable.

Yes, self-directed IRAs can hold Bitcoin, Ethereum, and other cryptocurrencies, but you must use a specialized custodian (e.g., Millennium Trust, Alto IRA with crypto partnerships). Key requirements: (1) Cryptocurrency must be held by custodian in custody wallet, never in your personal wallet. (2) All cryptocurrency must be purchased and sold through custodian-approved exchanges. (3) You cannot transfer or control crypto directly; custodian maintains control. (4) Custodian ensures proper tax reporting (Form 8949, capital gains calculations). (5) All gains are tax-deferred (Traditional) or tax-free (Roth) as long as held in IRA. Important: Direct personal possession of crypto in SDIRA-intended account constitutes prohibited distribution. Many IRA holders lost significant tax benefits when they held crypto personally instead of in custodial accounts. Use reputable custodians with strong security and insurance.

UBIT (Unrelated Business Income Tax) is a tax imposed on IRAs when they engage in "unrelated business activity." Related concepts: UDFI (Unrelated Debt-Financed Income) applies when debt finances a property. UBIT matters because: (1) Certain SDIRA activities generate taxable income within the IRA, reducing tax-deferral benefits. (2) Examples triggering UBIT: operating a business inside IRA (restaurant, consulting firm), mortgage-financed real estate (UDFI), and certain investment partnerships. (3) UBIT rate is the trust rate (currently up to 37% federal top marginal rate). (4) Filing requirement: If UBIT exceeds $1,000, you must file Form 990-T (UBIT return). (5) Landlord activities (passive real estate rentals) generally DO NOT trigger UBIT, even with mortgage debt. The key distinction: passive real estate rentals = no UBIT; active business operations or certain financing structures = UBIT. Work with SDIRA CPA to calculate UBIT before committing to complex investments.

Timeline varies: Account opening (new account): 3-7 business days. Custodian processes application, completes KYC (know-your-customer) verification, opens account. Account funding via direct contribution: 1-2 business days after deposit. Account funding via rollover: 2-4 weeks (depends on prior custodian processing time). Once funded, investment execution depends on asset type: direct stock purchase = same day; real estate property purchase = 30-90 days (due diligence, financing, closing); private equity deals = 15-45 days (documentation, legal review). For most investors, full process from application to first investment = 6-12 weeks. To accelerate, start custodian application immediately while identifying your investment simultaneously.

Yes, you can execute a 401(k) rollover to an SDIRA in two scenarios: (1) After leaving employment, your former employer's 401(k) allows distributions. You request a direct trustee-to-trustee transfer to SDIRA custodian, avoiding tax withholding. (2) If you're still employed but your plan permits in-service rollovers, you can move 401(k) funds to SDIRA while employed (less common but available in some plans). Process: Contact current 401(k) plan administrator and request rollover to SDIRA custodian (provide SDIRA custodian routing/account info). Funds transfer directly (no personal handling). Do NOT execute indirect rollover (funds sent to you personally) as 20% withholding applies and you have 60 days to reinvest. Key advantage: Rolling 401(k) to SDIRA unlocks alternative investment options (real estate, private equity, metals, crypto) not available in most 401(k)s. Rollover can be executed anytime; 401(k) balances can be as large as you accumulated (no annual limit like IRA contributions).

Prohibited transaction consequences are severe and permanent: (1) IRA disqualification: Entire SDIRA is deemed to have been distributed to you on the first day of the tax year when the prohibited transaction occurred. (2) Income tax: You owe federal income tax on the full fair market value of the IRA as of the disqualification date. (3) Penalty tax: You owe an additional 15% penalty tax on the entire IRA value. (4) Example: $200,000 SDIRA with prohibited transaction results in $200,000 taxable income + $30,000 (15%) penalty = $230,000 in taxes/penalties in single year. (5) State taxes: State income tax may also apply (varying by state). (6) No relief: Prohibited transactions are irrevocable; IRS does not grant relief, cannot "undo" transactions, and cannot restore SDIRA status. (7) Timing: Penalties apply retroactively to the first day of the tax year, not when discovered. Prevention is absolutely critical: Before any transaction, verify counterparty is not disqualified, document fair-market-value pricing, and maintain comprehensive records.

Yes, SDIRAs can use leverage, but ONLY through non-recourse loans (lender has no recourse to your personal assets if the property is foreclosed). Standard recourse loans, where you're personally liable, are prohibited transactions. Non-recourse loans allow: (1) SDIRA borrows $300,000 on $400,000 property. (2) SDIRA puts down $100,000 (down payment). (3) Lender's only remedy is foreclosure of the property; you have no personal liability. (4) Property title held in SDIRA trustee's name; loan also in SDIRA trustee's name (not yours personally). Advantages: Leverage amplifies appreciation (25% down, 75% debt = 4x leverage on gains). Disadvantages: UDFI/UBIT implications. Approximately 75% of annual rental income is UDFI (Unrelated Debt-Financed Income) subject to UBIT. However, mortgage principal reduction is not income. Many leveraged SDIRA real estate investments pay minimal UBIT because rent covers debt service but net income is limited. Calculate UBIT carefully before leveraging. Work with SDIRA-experienced lenders; non-recourse loans are a specialized market.

Neither is universally "better"—it depends on your situation. Solo 401(k) advantages: (1) Higher contribution limits ($69,000 in 2024 vs. $7,000 SDIRA cap). (2) Self-employed individuals earn $100,000+ income can contribute significantly more. (3) UBIT/UDFI treatment is more favorable for leveraged real estate; debt-financed properties generate UBIT only on debt repayment portion, not entire property. (4) Solo 401(k) loans permitted (borrow against account; prohibited in SDIRA). (5) Greater checkbook control and fewer custodian intermediation requirements. Solo 401(k) disadvantages: (1) Higher administrative burden (annual Form 5500-C filing, more IRS oversight). (2) Custodian fees often higher ($500-$1,500 vs. $200-$500 SDIRA). (3) Complexity requires experienced admin support. SDIRA advantages: (1) Simplicity; no annual tax forms (just standard IRA reporting). (2) Lower costs; fewer annual requirements. (3) Easy setup and management. SDIRA disadvantages: (1) Lower contribution limits; caps retirement savings. (2) UBIT/UDFI implications for leveraged real estate. (3) Less favorable tax treatment of debt-financed income. Recommendation: If earning $50,000-$100,000 self-employment income, use SDIRA for simplicity. If earning $100,000+, consider Solo 401(k) for higher contributions and better UBIT treatment.

Yes, SDIRAs can hold IRS-approved precious metals: (1) Gold bullion (coins, bars meeting minimum fineness—.995 for bars, .900 for coins). (2) Silver bullion (.999 fineness). (3) Platinum bullion (.9995 fineness). (4) Palladium bullion (.9995 fineness). Prohibited: collectible coins, numismatic coins, artwork, rare metals not meeting IRS fineness. Key requirements: (1) Metals must be stored in IRS-approved depository (vault provider like Brinks, Delaware Depository, LBMA-approved facilities). (2) You cannot take physical possession; depository maintains custody. (3) Storage fees apply ($100-$400 annually depending on value). (4) If you take possession personally, IRA makes a distribution and you owe taxes/penalties. Advantages: Tangible asset hedge against currency devaluation; tax-deferred growth. Disadvantages: Storage costs and depository fees; no income generation (unlike rental property or business investments). Good strategy for those believing in precious metals appreciation and wanting tax deferral.

Comprehensive documentation is critical for audit defense and proving compliance. Maintain: (1) Investment thesis and due diligence: business plans, market research, comparables analysis, appraisals. (2) Purchase documentation: offers, contracts, purchase agreements, closing statements, receipts. (3) Title documents: deeds, title insurance policies, title reports (real estate). (4) Loan documentation: promissory notes, loan agreements, non-recourse certification, lender statements. (5) Expense records: receipts for all property/business expenses (repairs, taxes, insurance, utilities). (6) Income documentation: rental agreements, tenant payment receipts, profit/loss statements (for businesses). (7) Account statements: custodian statements, annual valuations, transaction confirmations. (8) Tax documents: Form 5498 (annual), Form 1099-R (distributions), Form 990-T (UBIT if applicable). (9) Correspondence: all communications with custodian, vendors, partners, and advisors. (10) Decision documentation: records showing how investment was selected (preventing self-dealing accusations). Store digitally with backups; maintain copies for 7+ years (IRS audit period). Good documentation proves fair-market-value pricing, absence of prohibited transactions, and compliance with SDIRA rules.

Form 1099-R (annual): Custodian files Form 1099-R for any distributions (withdrawals) from SDIRA. You receive copy and must report distributions on your tax return. For Traditional SDIRA, distributions are fully taxable. For Roth SDIRA, distributions are tax-free if qualified (age 59½, 5-year rule satisfied). Form 990-T (UBIT return, if applicable): If SDIRA generates UBIT (Unrelated Business Income Tax) exceeding $1,000 in a tax year, you must file Form 990-T with the IRS. This applies to debt-financed real estate (mortgage triggers UBIT on portion of income attributable to debt) or active business operations. UBIT rate is trust rate (up to 37%). Filing deadline: Form 990-T due by March 15 (not April 15). Failure to file can trigger penalties. Work with SDIRA-experienced CPA to calculate UBIT and file required forms.

Yes, you can have multiple SDIRAs. Many investors maintain: (1) Traditional SDIRA for tax-deferred growth on conservative investments. (2) Roth SDIRA for tax-free growth on high-risk/high-growth investments. (3) SEP SDIRA or other variants if self-employed. Contribution limits: Combined annual contributions across all IRAs cannot exceed $7,000 ($8,000 if 50+) for 2024. So if you have Traditional + Roth SDIRA, your combined contribution is limited to $7,000 total (not $7,000 each). Advantage: Diversification by account type (Traditional/Roth) allows tax optimization. However, multiple custodians mean multiple fees. Consider consolidating unless specific tax planning justifies multiple accounts.
