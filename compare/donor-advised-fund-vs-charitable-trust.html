Donor-Advised Fund vs Charitable Trust

Use a DAF to give and walk away. Use a CRT when the same assets must still pay you.

When a donor-advised fund is enough for bunching appreciated stock versus when a charitable remainder trust is the vehicle because you still need an income stream.

Executive summary

Appreciated public stock is the usual fuel. Selling it yourself realizes gain. Giving it to a public charity or a donor-advised fund generally avoids that gain and gives you a fair-market-value deduction, limited by AGI, with a five-year carryforward. The fund can grant to charities later. You do not get the money back.

A charitable remainder unitrust or annuity trust is a different contract. The trust can sell the stock without the gain hitting your return in the year of sale. You take a unitrust or annuity payout, often 5 percent or more of a CRUT. The IRS requires a remainder that is worth at least 10 percent at funding. Legal and actuarial work is real cost. The remainder is locked to charity.

People open a CRT because a slide said income for life, then they fund $150,000 and spend $12,000 on documents. A DAF would have granted the same dollars with a form. People also dump $2 million of founder stock into a DAF and then wonder why they have no paycheck from those shares. That second household needed a trust, or they needed to give only a slice.

Name the job. Bunch deductions in a high-income year. Remove a concentrated position. Replace a paycheck from the gifted principal. Only the last job points at a CRT first.

A donor-advised fund is a giving account. You contribute cash or appreciated assets, take a deduction subject to AGI limits, and recommend grants over time. A charitable remainder trust is a split-interest vehicle. You transfer assets, keep an income stream for life or a term, and leave the remainder to charity. The deduction is the present value of that remainder, not the full transfer. If you do not need income from the gifted assets, the fund is usually the cleaner file.

Written for households with appreciated stock or a business-sale gain who want a charitable deduction and are split between simplicity and lifetime income.

When Donor-Advised Fund tends to win

A donor-advised fund wins when you want a deduction this year, you can live without those assets, and you want to recommend grants later without trust administration.

When Charitable Remainder Trust tends to win

A charitable remainder trust wins when the block is large, you need a lifetime or term payout from it, and the remainder value and setup cost still make the deduction and the income plan worth the contract.

Where people lose money

Funding a charitable remainder trust for a mid-five-figure gift because the income-for-life phrase sounded sophisticated, then paying setup costs that dwarf the extra planning value.

Decision framework

If you do not need a paycheck from the donated assets, start with a donor-advised fund. Open a CRT only when income, size, and the remainder test all clear.

  1. List the assets you might give, the basis, the unrealized gain, and AGI for this year.

  2. Write the cash you still need from those assets over the next 10 years. If the answer is none, stay with a DAF unless an estate lawyer has a separate reason.

  3. Compute the AGI percentage limits for cash versus appreciated property and the five-year carryforward.

  4. If a CRT is still in play, ask counsel for a 7520-rate remainder calculation at a 5 percent and a 6 percent payout, including legal cost.

  5. Decide who the remainder charity is, and whether you want grant flexibility through a DAF named as the CRT remainder.

Worked example

Age 61, AGI $410,000, $720,000 of public stock with $95,000 of basis. They want about $40,000 a year of extra cash and they already give about $20,000 a year to the same three charities.

  • A gift of long-term appreciated stock to a public DAF is limited to 30 percent of AGI this year, or $123,000, with a five-year carryforward for the rest
  • Selling the full $720,000 themselves would realize about $625,000 of gain
  • A 5.5 percent CRUT on $720,000 would target about $39,600 of year-one payout. Remainder value must clear the 10 percent test at the 7520 rate then in effect.
  • CRT legal and actuarial cost is modeled at $12,000. A DAF account has no setup fee in this model.

Donor-Advised Fund outcome

They contribute $123,000 of stock to the DAF this year, avoid gain on that slice, and deduct $123,000 subject to itemizing. They can contribute more in later years. They still own $597,000 of the stock if they need the $40,000 paycheck from their own portfolio. Grants can stay at $20,000 a year from the fund.

Charitable Remainder Trust outcome

They fund a $720,000 CRUT, take a remainder-based deduction that is much smaller than $720,000, and start a ~$39,600 payout. The trust can sell and diversify without the $625,000 gain hitting their 1040 in the sale year. They no longer own the $720,000. Setup cost is $12,000 and the remainder is no longer theirs.

What the scenario shows

Give the slice you can live without to a DAF. Use a CRT when the whole block must both leave your name and keep paying you. Do not buy a trust for a gift a fund can hold in a week.

Evidence and documentation standards

If the evidence package is weak, the strategy that looks better on paper usually underperforms in practice.

Eligibility and qualification proof Pull basis, holding period, and AGI for this year before you transfer anything. You name a DAF as the CRT remainder so you can keep grant flexibility after the income term.
Economic substantiation Write the annual cash you still need from these assets. A closely held note or restricted stock that a DAF sponsor will not accept, which can force a trust or a pre-gift cleanup.
Contemporaneous logs and operating records If using a DAF, confirm the sponsor accepts the asset and the AGI limit on this gift. You need a charitable lead trust or a private foundation for a different job. Neither is this comparison.
Governance artifacts and approvals If using a CRT, get a remainder calculation and a fee quote before you sign. State income tax on CRT payouts and NIIT on net investment income change the paycheck you thought was clean.
Annual review archive Store the contribution receipt or trust instrument with the return that claims the deduction. Without annual review data, the same mistakes are repeated in later filing years.

Failure modes and mitigations

These are the practical breakdowns that turn a valid strategy into an expensive cleanup project.

Edge cases that change the decision

  • You name a DAF as the CRT remainder so you can keep grant flexibility after the income term.
  • A closely held note or restricted stock that a DAF sponsor will not accept, which can force a trust or a pre-gift cleanup.
  • You need a charitable lead trust or a private foundation for a different job. Neither is this comparison.
  • State income tax on CRT payouts and NIIT on net investment income change the paycheck you thought was clean.

Avoid Donor-Advised Fund if

  • You need a paycheck from the same shares you plan to give.
  • The sponsor will not accept the asset you actually own.
  • You want a legally binding remainder to one institution with trust terms your counsel drafts.

Avoid Charitable Remainder Trust if

  • The gift is under a few hundred thousand and legal fees will dominate.
  • You do not want an income stream and you like recommending grants year by year.
  • The remainder test fails at a payout high enough to cover your budget.

90-day plan

Decide early, set the guardrails, then keep the records as you go.

  1. Days 0 to 30: decision and controls

    • Pull basis, holding period, and AGI for this year before you transfer anything.
    • Write the annual cash you still need from these assets.

  2. Days 31 to 60: execution and documentation

    • If using a DAF, confirm the sponsor accepts the asset and the AGI limit on this gift.
    • If using a CRT, get a remainder calculation and a fee quote before you sign.

  3. Days 61 to 90: validation and advisor packet

    • Store the contribution receipt or trust instrument with the return that claims the deduction.
    • Run post-implementation review, compare projected vs actual results, and adjust the playbook for next quarter.

Questions to ask your CPA

  • At my AGI, how much appreciated stock can I deduct this year to a DAF, and what carries forward?
  • If we fund a CRUT at 5 percent and at 6 percent, what remainder deduction do the tables produce at my age?
  • Will my state tax CRT payouts in a way that changes the paycheck I am counting on?
  • Should the CRT remainder be a DAF so my family can recommend grants later?

What to bring

  • A one-page objective memo clarifying what "winning" means for this decision (Donor-Advised Fund vs Charitable Remainder Trust).
  • Baseline and alternative math model with all assumptions clearly listed.
  • Supporting evidence folder for qualification, valuations, logs, and policy records.
  • Risk memo covering edge cases, red flags, and fallback plan if assumptions fail.
  • Annual review checklist showing what will be re-evaluated before next filing cycle.

Questions people ask

Do I get a deduction for the full amount I put into a charitable remainder trust?

No. You deduct the present value of the remainder that will go to charity, using IRS tables, your age or term, the payout, and the 7520 rate. The income interest is not a charitable deduction.

Can a donor-advised fund pay me income the way a CRT does?

No. Once the assets are in the fund, grants go to charities. If you still need income from those dollars, keep the assets or use a split-interest trust. Do not expect a DAF sponsor to send you a paycheck.

What is the 10 percent remainder test?

A charitable remainder trust must be set so the present value of the remainder is at least 10 percent of the funding amount at inception. A high payout on a young donor can fail that test. That is a math gate, not a taste preference.

Can I bunch several years of giving into a DAF in a high-income year?

Yes, that is a common use. You contribute more than you plan to grant this year, take the deduction now subject to AGI limits, and recommend grants later. A CRT is a weaker bunching tool because the deduction is only the remainder value.
