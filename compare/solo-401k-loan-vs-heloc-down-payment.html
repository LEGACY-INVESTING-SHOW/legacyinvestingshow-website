Solo 401(k) Loan vs HELOC for Down Payment Funding

Funding speed can hide long-term drag.

A comparison for business owners and high earners funding a down payment: Solo 401(k) loan vs HELOC, focused on risk, flexibility, and real cost.

Executive summary

When a good deal shows up, the down payment is usually the bottleneck.

A Solo 401(k) loan feels clean because you are borrowing from yourself. A HELOC feels flexible because the bank money is there when you need it.

The decision is not just interest rate. It is risk, timing, and what happens if life gets messy.

Both options can work. Both can also create a mess if you ignore job risk, liquidity needs, and the true cost of pulling leverage from the wrong place.

Written for business owners funding a down payment without blowing up liquidity or retirement momentum.

When Solo 401(k) Loan tends to win

A Solo 401(k) loan can win when repayment capacity is strong and you need speed without bank underwriting friction.

When HELOC tends to win

A HELOC can win when you need flexible access, can handle rate variability, and want to preserve retirement account momentum.

Where people lose money

Treating a 401(k) loan as cheap money and ignoring opportunity cost and repayment risk if income drops.

Decision framework

Start with worst-case scenarios. If the deal goes sideways or income drops, which funding source keeps you alive?

  1. Define the down payment amount, timeline, and how quickly you will refinance or repay.

  2. Stress test income risk and liquidity needs for 12 months.

  3. Compare total costs including fees, rate resets, and lost market growth.

  4. Set a repayment plan before you draw funds.

  5. Document the funding decision and revisit after closing.

Worked example

Self-employed owner needs $120k down payment in 21 days for an investment property.

  • Solo 401(k) balance allows a loan large enough
  • HELOC is approved but rate is variable
  • Plan includes refinance in 9 to 12 months

Solo 401(k) Loan outcome

401(k) loan provides speed, but repayment schedule reduces flexibility if the deal takes longer to stabilize.

HELOC outcome

HELOC preserves retirement growth, but a rate spike can change the cost mid-stream.

What the scenario shows

The right answer depends on your income stability and how quickly the property becomes self-sustaining.

Failure modes and mitigations

These are the practical breakdowns that turn a valid strategy into an expensive cleanup project.

Failure mode Mitigation control
Income drops and loan repayment becomes a monthly stress. Solo 401(k) Loan and HELOC should only be implemented after an explicit documentation standard is agreed with your advisor.
HELOC rate spikes during renovation or vacancy. Replace assumptions with verifiable evidence (contracts, logs, policy docs, or third-party support).
Solo 401(k) Loan misuse: Your income is volatile and repayment pressure is risky. Use Solo 401(k) Loan only when the qualification gate is clearly met and documented before filing.
HELOC misuse: You cannot handle variable rates. Use HELOC only when the execution process can be maintained consistently during the year.

Edge cases that change the decision

  • Income drops and loan repayment becomes a monthly stress.
  • HELOC rate spikes during renovation or vacancy.
  • Refinance takes longer than planned.
  • Liquidity is tied up and emergency reserves are thin.

Avoid Solo 401(k) Loan if

  • Your income is volatile and repayment pressure is risky.
  • You cannot tolerate missing market growth in the account.
  • You are using the loan as a long-term crutch.

Avoid HELOC if

  • You cannot handle variable rates.
  • Your HELOC terms are weak or fees are heavy.
  • You are already tight on monthly cash flow.

90-day plan

Decide early, set the guardrails, then keep the records as you go.

  1. Days 0 to 30: decision and controls

    • Confirm loan limits and plan rules before committing.
    • Price the HELOC with worst-case rate scenario.

  2. Days 31 to 60: execution and documentation

    • Set repayment plan and refinance timeline.
    • Protect emergency reserves.

  3. Days 61 to 90: validation and advisor packet

    • Review after closing and adjust plan if the deal shifts.
    • Run post-implementation review, compare projected vs actual results, and adjust the playbook for next quarter.

Questions to ask your CPA

  • What is the real total cost including opportunity cost?
  • What happens if refinance takes 6 months longer?
  • How does this impact my retirement plan and tax picture?
  • What guardrails should I set before drawing funds?

What to bring

  • A one-page objective memo clarifying what "winning" means for this decision (Solo 401(k) Loan vs HELOC).
  • Baseline and alternative math model with all assumptions clearly listed.
  • Supporting evidence folder for qualification, valuations, logs, and policy records.
  • Risk memo covering edge cases, red flags, and fallback plan if assumptions fail.
  • Annual review checklist showing what will be re-evaluated before next filing cycle.

Questions people ask

Is borrowing from a Solo 401(k) always a good idea?

No. It can be a tool, but it adds repayment pressure and opportunity cost.

Is a HELOC too risky because rates are variable?

Not always. It depends on your cash flow, your rate tolerance, and your backup plan.

What is the best guardrail?

Protect emergency reserves and define a payoff plan before you borrow.
