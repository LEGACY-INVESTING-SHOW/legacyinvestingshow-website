Executive Summary

When a good deal shows up, the down payment is usually the bottleneck.

A Solo 401(k) loan feels clean because you are borrowing from yourself. A HELOC feels flexible because the bank money is there when you need it.

The decision is not just interest rate. It is risk, timing, and what happens if life gets messy.

Bottom line: Both options can work. Both can also create a mess if you ignore job risk, liquidity needs, and the true cost of pulling leverage from the wrong place.

Decision Framework (Execution-First)

Start with worst-case scenarios. If the deal goes sideways or income drops, which funding source keeps you alive?

  1. Define the down payment amount, timeline, and how quickly you will refinance or repay.
  2. Stress test income risk and liquidity needs for 12 months.
  3. Compare total costs including fees, rate resets, and lost market growth.
  4. Set a repayment plan before you draw funds.
  5. Document the funding decision and revisit after closing.

Worked Example (Scenario Model)

Profile: Self-employed owner needs $120k down payment in 21 days for an investment property.

  • Solo 401(k) balance allows a loan large enough
  • HELOC is approved but rate is variable
  • Plan includes refinance in 9 to 12 months

Solo 401(k) Loan outcome

401(k) loan provides speed, but repayment schedule reduces flexibility if the deal takes longer to stabilize.

HELOC outcome

HELOC preserves retirement growth, but a rate spike can change the cost mid-stream.

Scenario takeaway: The right answer depends on your income stability and how quickly the property becomes self-sustaining.

Failure Modes and Mitigations

These are not hypothetical. They are the practical breakdowns that repeatedly turn a valid strategy into an expensive cleanup project:

Failure Mode Mitigation Control
Income drops and loan repayment becomes a monthly stress. Solo 401(k) Loan and HELOC should only be implemented after an explicit documentation standard is agreed with your advisor.
HELOC rate spikes during renovation or vacancy. Replace assumptions with verifiable evidence (contracts, logs, policy docs, or third-party support).
Solo 401(k) Loan misuse: Your income is volatile and repayment pressure is risky. Use Solo 401(k) Loan only when the qualification gate is clearly met and documented before filing.
HELOC misuse: You cannot handle variable rates. Use HELOC only when the execution process can be maintained consistently during the year.

Edge Cases That Change the Decision

  • Income drops and loan repayment becomes a monthly stress.
  • HELOC rate spikes during renovation or vacancy.
  • Refinance takes longer than planned.
  • Liquidity is tied up and emergency reserves are thin.

When Not to Use This Strategy

Avoid Solo 401(k) Loan if...

  • Your income is volatile and repayment pressure is risky.
  • You cannot tolerate missing market growth in the account.
  • You are using the loan as a long-term crutch.

Avoid HELOC if...

  • You cannot handle variable rates.
  • Your HELOC terms are weak or fees are heavy.
  • You are already tight on monthly cash flow.
