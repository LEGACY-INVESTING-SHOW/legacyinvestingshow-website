Solo 401(k) Loan vs HELOC for Down Payment (Tax + Liquidity Edge Cases)

A practical financing comparison for self-employed buyers funding down payments under liquidity pressure.

Quick Verdict: Depends on income volatility and collateral flexibility. This is a capital-stack decision, not a rate-shopping exercise. You need to model repayment risk, business volatility, and opportunity cost together before selecting a funding source.

When Solo 401(k) Loan Usually Wins

Best when retirement plan rules permit loan terms and business cash flow supports repayment certainty.

When HELOC Usually Wins

Best when collateral capacity exists and loan flexibility is more valuable than retirement-plan borrowing constraints.

Most Common Mistake

Optimizing for the biggest deduction headline instead of the most reliable after-tax outcome for your actual facts.

Worked Example (Scenario Model)

Profile: Self-employed owner needs $110k down payment with variable quarterly business income.

  • Solo 401(k) loan available under plan terms
  • HELOC has variable rate sensitivity
  • Business cash flow can dip 30% in weak quarter

Solo 401(k) Loan Outcome

Solo 401(k) loan offers predictable structure but creates tighter repayment stress under income dip scenario.

HELOC Outcome

HELOC offers draw flexibility but rate variability can increase carrying cost materially.

Scenario takeaway: Stress tests, not nominal rates, should decide this choice.

Edge Cases That Change the Decision

  • Business income shock makes plan-loan repayment cadence difficult.
  • Rate spikes turn HELOC from tactical bridge into long-term cost drag.
  • Borrower underestimates closing and reserve liquidity needs.
  • Down payment strategy ignores post-close capex requirements.

When Not to Use This Strategy

Avoid Solo 401(k) Loan If...

  • Income is highly unstable quarter to quarter.
  • You cannot sustain strict repayment cadence.
  • You are near retirement and cannot absorb sequence risk.

Avoid HELOC If...

  • You cannot tolerate variable-rate uncertainty.
  • Collateral position is fragile.
  • You need fixed repayment predictability.

30-Day Implementation Checklist

  • Run downside-case 12-month repayment stress test.
  • Model opportunity cost and debt cost under multiple rate paths.
  • Set minimum post-close liquidity reserve threshold.
  • Define exit/refinance trigger criteria before borrowing.
  • Document funding choice with risk controls and contingency steps.

Questions to Ask Your CPA/Advisor

  • What repayment stress threshold makes this financing unsafe?
  • How should we size liquidity reserves post-closing?
  • What rate scenario breaks HELOC viability for my deal?
  • What contingency plan should we pre-approve if income drops?

Frequently Asked Questions

Is borrowing from a Solo 401(k) always cheaper?

Not necessarily. Cost and risk depend on repayment reliability and opportunity cost tradeoffs.

Is HELOC always too risky in variable-rate environments?

Not always. It can work with defined rate guardrails and strong liquidity buffers.

Can I combine both?

Sometimes, but complexity and risk rise quickly and require disciplined planning.
